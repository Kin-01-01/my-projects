Abstract 
Metal-air batteries have much higher theoretical energy density than metal ion 
batteries, however, their application is hampered by the sluggish oxygen reduction

reaction (ORR) and the oxygen evolution reaction (OER). As such, electrocatalysis is 
employed to facilitate the ORR and OER. Despite enormous efforts, real time imaging 
of the electrocatalysis during the ORR and OER processes remain elusive. 
Furthermore, it is controversial whether or not electrocatalysis is necessary in the 
Na-O2 battery. Here we show the first in-situ imaging of the operation of the 
electrocatalysis in a Na-O2 battery in an advanced aberration corrected environmental 
transmission electron microscopy (ETEM). In the Au-coated MnO2 nanowire air 
cathode, the ORR is characterized by the formation of NaO2 nano bubbles nucleated 
from the Au catalysts, causing an 18 times volume increase on the surface of the 
MnO2 nanowires; the NaO2 quickly disproportionated to Na2O2 and O2, causing 
collapse of the NaO2 nano bubbles. In contrast, no ORR took place in the bare MnO2 
nanowire cathode; instead, the MnO2 nanowires only swelled 217% as a result of the 
Na+ intercalation. The results provide not only new insight into the Au-catalyzed 
oxygen chemistry in the Na-O2 battery, but also an atom-level characterization 
technique to evaluate the electrocatalysis in the metal air batteries.

1.Introduction 
Alkaline metal (especially Li and Na) air batteries have attracted worldwide 
attentions due to their much higher theoretical energy density than that of the 
state-of-the-art metal ion batteries [1-9]. However, the practical application of metal 

=== PAGE 4 ===
3 
 
air batteries is plagued by their poor cyclability, low rate performance and low 
round-trip efficiency caused by the sluggish oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) and 
oxygen evolution reaction (OER) kinetics in the air cathodes. As such, noble metals 
such as Au, Pd, Pt, Ru, etc.[10-12] and transition metal oxides such as MnO2, Co3O4, 
Fe2O3, CuO, etc.[13-15] are used as the electrocatalysts to facilitate the sluggish ORR 
and OER in the lithium air batteries [16, 17]. In contrast, carbon based electrodes 
without catalysts exhibit good performance when they are used as air cathode in 
Na-O2 batteries [18-20]. Lower over-potential for both ORR and OER as well as 
better cyclability are observed in carbon cathode based Na-O2 batteries but not in 
Li-O2 batteries, rendering many researchers to believe that electrocatalysts are not 
needed, or the carbon based materials themselves can act as efficient electrocatalysts, 
in the liquid non-aqueous electrolytes based Na-O2 batteries [21-23]. Others have 
used the noble metals and transition metal oxides as the electrocatalysis in the Na-O2 
batteries [24-26]. Question then arises as to whether or not electrocatalysis is 
necessary in the Na-O2 batteries? If yes, how does the electrocatalysis process, 
including the structure evolution of the catalysts as well as the nucleation and growth 
of the discharge products, proceed in the Na-O2 batteries? The advent of aberration 
corrected environmental transmission electron microscopy (ETEM) recently renders 
in-situ studies of the metal-O2 batteries a reality, which may shed light on the critical 
problems in the Na-O2 batteries. 
2. Results 
To elucidate the electrocatalytic effect of Au nanoparticles in the metal oxygen 

=== PAGE 5 ===
4 
 
battery, an all solid-state Na-O2 nano battery comprised of Au nanoparticles coated 
MnO2 nanowires (Au/MnO2 NWs) air cathode, a naturally formed Na2O electrolyte, 
and Na anode was assembled in an aberration-corrected ETEM operating under an O2 
gas environment (Fig. 1a). Coating of Au nanoparticles on the surface of the 
hollandite α-MnO2 nanowires was achieved via ion sputtering of Au in vacuum (Fig. 
S1a). Au nanoparticles with size of < 5 nm were uniformly dispersed on the surface of 
the MnO2 nanowires (Fig. S1a-b, e). High resolution TEM (HRTEM) images (Fig. 
S1c, f) and Fast Fourier Transformation (FFT, insets in Fig. S1c, f) indicate that the 
nanowire is hollandite α-MnO2 (JCPDS card No. 44-0141) with a growth direction of 
[001]. Fig. S1d, g shows HRTEM images of the Au nanoparticles with [011] and [001] 
zone arises on the MnO2 nanowire. Due to the use of KMnO4 as the precursor, the K+ 
ion should occupy the 2a Wyckoff position in the hollandite α-MnO2 [27] to stabilize 
its large 2 × 2 tunnels. During discharge, the hollandite α-MnO2 with axial 2 × 2 and 1 
× 1 atomic tunnels offer fast Na+ transportation pathways (Fig. S1h). The small radial 
tunnels (Fig. S1i) offer the radial Na+ ion transportation channels between the surface 
and interior of the nanowires [28].  
The time-resolved annular dark-field (ADF) images of the morphology evolution 
of the discharge products on the Au/MnO2 cathode are shown in Fig. 1b-g and Movie 
S1-3. Upon applying a negative potential to the Au/MnO2 air cathode against the Na 
anode, the Au/MnO2 nanowire swelled slightly; at 205 s (Fig. 1c), a bubble-like 
conformal coating layer with lighter contrast than that of the Au/MnO2 NW cathode 
emerged at the Au/MnO2 NW surface near the right contact, which then propagated 

=== PAGE 6 ===
5 
 
along the NW, causing an 18 times volume increase of the NW. The lighter contrast of 
the conformal coating layer than the central Au/MnO2 NW indicates that the discharge 
products have lower density than that of the Au/MnO2 NW. Concurrent with the 
emergence of the bubble-like discharge products at the NW surface, the Au coating 
layer on the NW surface was lifted up the NW surface, blended with the discharge 
products, aggregated to larger Au nanoparticles dispersing in the discharge products. 
The bubbles eventually collapsed after prolonged discharge time, causing volume 
shrinkage of the discharge products. Upon reversing the potential, the discharge 
products disintegrated, suggesting the electrochemical decomposition of the discharge 
products (Fig. 1h-i). However, the decomposition of the discharge products was 
incomplete, with some large nanoparticles resided on the surface of the aggregated Au 
nanoparticles, and they maintained an intimate contact even after extensive charging 
time. The HRTEM image (Fig. 1j) indicates that the residue discharge product 
maintained nearly an epitaxial relationship with the Au nanoparticles after the 
charging process, suggesting that these residue discharge products nucleated or grew 
along the Au nanoparticles. It is noted that the structure of the MnO2 NW remained 
intact after the ORR and OER, recovering to nearly its initial diameter and length (Fig. 
1i). In the meantime, the aggregated Au nanoparticles dispersed in the discharge 
products receded back to the NW surface (Fig. 1i). The phase structure and chemical 
composition of the discharge products were investigated by electron diffraction 
pattern (EDP) and electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS). The EDP from the 
initial Au/MnO2 NW shows sharp diffraction spots superimposed on diffraction rings

(Fig. 2a, b) which are indexed as the hollandite α-MnO2 and the face-center-cubic Au, 
respectively. The EDP of the bubble-like discharge products near the reaction front 
also displays sparse diffraction spots superimposed on diffraction rings (Fig. 2a, c), 
being indexed as NaO2, Na2O2, Na0.5MnO2 and the residue Au. Diffraction rings were 
observed in the EDP from the shrunken region (Fig. 2a, d), which are indexed as 
mainly Na2O2, Au and Na0.5MnO2. The EELS spectra from different regions of the 
Au/MnO2 NW after discharge are shown in Fig. 2e, f. In the low-loss spectra (Fig. 2e), 
the major plasmon peak shifts from 27.4 eV (red plot in Fig. 2e) of the pristine 
Au/MnO2 to 25.2 eV after discharge (blue plot in Fig. 2e), suggesting the formation of 
sodium oxides or superoxides. The core-loss spectra (Fig. 2f) indicate the presence of 
Mn, O in the pristine NW (red plot), and Na, Mn, O elements in the discharged 
segments (blue plot) of the NW, and no clear change was discerned in the Mn L2,3 
profile of the Au/MnO2 before and after discharging, suggesting the stability of the 
[MnO6] octahedra in the 2 × 2 atomic tunnels and that the α-MnO2 mainly acts as 
ionic transport tunnels during the discharging process. Acquisition of EELS spectra 
from the discharge products was extremely difficult due to the beam sensitivity of the 
discharge products which disappeared almost instantly once the electron beam was 
placed on it. Nevertheless, weak signals were collected (green plots in Fig. 2e-f), in 
which the low-loss spectrum shows a weak plasmon peak at 15.2 eV with a shoulder 
at 8.2 eV, and the core-loss spectrum shows the presence weak O-K and Na-K edges, 
confirming the formation sodium oxide or superoxide. The EDP and EELS of the 
charge products (Fig. S2) indicate that the residues after electrochemically

To illustrate the catalytic effect of the Au nanoparticles, discharge experiments 
with bare hollandite α-MnO2 nanowire as air cathode were also conducted in the 
ETEM under the same experimental conditions as that of the Au/MnO2 air cathode 
(Fig. 3 and Movie S4). In a sharp contrast to the Au/MnO2 air cathode, the bare MnO2 
air cathode showed only 217 % volume increment (diameter increased from 41 to 73 
nm) with a thin film coating (10 nm) on the surface of the MnO2 NW (Fig. 3a-c), and 
formation of bubble-like NaO2 discharge products observed in the former, were absent 
in the latter, indicating a clear electrocatalytic effect of the Au nanoparticles on the 
formation of NaO2 via one-electron transfer ORR. No clear change was observed 
during the charging process under positive bias even after 3293 s (Fig. 3d-f). The EDP 
from the pristine NW is indexed as the hollandite α-MnO2 (Fig. 3g). EDP indexed to 
mainly Na0.5MnO2 was observed in the MnO2 NW cathode after discharge and charge 
(Fig. 3h-i), indicating the occurrence of the dominant intercalation of Na+ ions. EELS 
analysis supports the EDP results. The low-loss EELS spectrum from the discharged 
NW displays a main plasmon loss peak at 23.4 eV (Fig. 3j, green plot), which is 4.1 
eV lower than that of the pristine MnO2 NW (Fig. 3j, red plot), suggesting a lower 
density of the intercalated NW. The core-loss spectra before (Fig. 3k, red plot) and 
after discharge (Fig. 3k, green plot) show similar profile, suggesting the intercalation 
nature of the Na+ with no electron transfer. The EELS spectra from the charged NW 
(Fig. 3j-k, blue plots) are similar to that of the discharged NW. This result indicates 
that mainly intercalation of Na+ into the 2 × 2 tunnels of the pure α-MnO2, rather than 

=== PAGE 9 ===
8 
 
the ORR, occurred during the discharging process, which is similar to the sodiation of 
hollandite α-MnO2 nanowire in sodium ion batteries without gas [29, 30], although 
the cathode was exposed in the 1 mbar O2 atmosphere in our experiments. The 
comparison experiments prove that major ORR took place only at the presence of Au 
nanoparticles at the MnO2 NW, indicating a clear catalytic effect of the Au 
nanoparticles.   
The nucleation and growth of NaO2 from Au nanoparticles during discharge 
were captured in real time. As shown in Fig. 4a and Movie S5, the distribution density 
of the Au nanoparticles on the upper side is clearly lower than that on the lower side 
of the MnO2 NW. Aggregation of Au nanoparticles was observed before the 
nucleation and growth of the NaO2 discharge products (Fig. 4a, b). Once the Au 
nanoparticle reached about 20 nm, NaO2 began to nucleate and grow around the Au 
nanoparticle (Fig. 4a-e), and a NaO2 nanoparticle with size of 91 nm was formed in 
only 18 s (Fig. 4e). After 193 s, a string of bubble-like NaO2 were formed mainly at 
the lower side with more Au particles (Fig. S3). Structure evolution of the discharge 
product was monitored by in-situ EDP. As shown in Fig. 4f-g and Movie S5, sharp 
NaO2 diffraction spots (marked by pink circles) appeared during the nucleation and 
initial growth of NaO2 on the aggregated Au nanoparticles via one electron ORR as 
Na+ + e- + O2 → NaO2. The NaO2 quickly dispropotionated to Na2O2 and O2, as 
evidenced by the appearance of diffraction rings from Na2O2 (Fig. 4h).  
To investigate the effect of the aggregation of Au nanoparticles during the first 
ORR process in the catalytic behavior of the Au/MnO2 cathode, we performed the 

=== PAGE 10 ===
9 
 
second ORR process after the first ORR/OER cycle. As shown in Fig. S4, after the 
first ORR, the NW swelled significantly due to the production of NaO2 and Na2O2 at 
the NW surface (Fig. S4a-c). After the first OER, the diameter of the NW shrank 
slightly due to the decomposition of the Na2O2 (Fig. S4d-e). During the second ORR, 
the volume of the NW segment without fresh Au catalyst did not change obviously, 
but the NW segment with fresh Au catalyst expanded dramatically due to the 
production of NaO2 and Na2O2 at the NW surface (Fig. S4f-g), suggesting that the 
aggregated Au lost its catalytic capability due to possibly its larger size and the 
surface passivation by Na2O. 
3.Discussion  
Based on the above results, we propose a reaction mechanism as shown in Fig. 
5a. Na+ crossed over the Na2O electrolyte and intercalated into the 8h sites of the 2 × 
2 tunnels in the hollandite α-MnO2. The Na+ transported axially along the MnO2 NW 
via the 2 × 2 tunnels to form Na0.5MnO2 and then was extracted radially to the NW 
surface to react with the superoxide ions (
-
2
O ) formed on the surface of the Au 
nanoparticles via one electron ORR, i.e., Na+ + e- + O2 → NaO2, to form bubble like 
NaO2 products. The NaO2 quickly dispropotionated to Na2O2 and O2 via NaO2 → 
Na2O2 + O2. The disproportionation of NaO2 on Au/MnO2 here, which differs from 
the stable cubic NaO2 formed on the CNT cathode of solid state Na-O2 battery in 
ETEM [31], and the reported cubic NaO2 with good chemical stability in 
solution-based Na-O2 battery via solution-mediate mechanism [32] imply that the Au 
or MnO2 promote the chemical decomposition of NaO2. The occurrence of ORR only 

=== PAGE 11 ===
10 
 
on the Au/MnO2 NW surface but not on bare α-MnO2 cathode suggests the formation 
of 
-
2
O on the surface of the Au nanoparticles, implying the high electrocatalytic 
activity of the Au nanoparticles (Fig. 5b-c). Different from the in-situ imaging ORR 
process of the Na-O2 battery with CuO cathode in which the metal Cu derived from 
conversion reaction was mixed in the discharge products [33], the stability of the 
MnO2 NW skeleton in Au/MnO2 cathode more clearly demonstrate the catalytic effect 
of metallic Au nanoparticles during the ORR process. Moreover, the bubble-like NaO2 
formed on the aggregated Au nanoparticles, indicating that the Au nanoparticles also 
acted as the nuclei for NaO2. The results provide direct evidence for electrocatalysis 
in the ORR of the Na-O2 batteries, offering a novel strategy to evaluate the 
electrocatalysis by in-situ electron microscopy. 
4.Conclusions 
In-situ ETEM studies of the Na-O2 batteries reveal an apparent Au nanoparticles 
catalyzed ORR, namely, in the Au-coated MnO2 air cathode, bubble-like NaO2 formed 
first on the NW surface, which then dispropotionated to Na2O2 and O2 during 
discharge. In contrast, ORR did not occur in the bare MnO2 air cathode during 
discharge under the same experimental conditions as that of the Au/MnO2 NW. The 
formation of NaO2 on the Au/MnO2 NW surface caused an 18 times volume increase, 
while the structure of the MnO2 NW remained nearly intact after cycling. The results 
provide new insight into the Au-catalyzed oxygen chemistry in the Na-O2 battery and 
a novel technology to evaluate electrocatalysis in metal air batteries in real time by 
in-situ electron microscopy.

5.Methods 
5.1 Materials Preparation 
The α-MnO2 nanowires was prepared via a solution growth method as the 
following. A 0.5 g KMnO4 solution and a 0.25 g MnCl2 solution were mixed with a 70 
mL solution with strongly stirring for 18 h. The mixture was transferred into the 100 
mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and was heated at 180 °C for 12 h. The final 
α-MnO2 was obtained after centrifugalization, washing and drying. 
5.2 In-situ nano battery setup in the ETEM  
The nano battery was constructed through a two-probe configuration in a Cs-corrected 
ETEM (FEI, Titan G2, 300 kV). MnO2 nanowires were glued to an aluminum rod 
with conductive epoxy firstly. The Au nanoparticles were coated on the glued MnO2 
NW via ion sputtering, and the Au-coated MnO2 NWs were used as the working 
cathode. Na metal scratched on a tungsten tip inside a glove box filled with argon gas 
was used as the reference and counter electrode. The native Na2O layer formed on the 
surface of the Na metal was used as a solid electrolyte for Na+ transportation. Both 
Au/MnO2 and Na electrodes were mounted onto a TEM-STM (Scanning Tunneling 
Microscopy) holder (Pico Femto FE-F20 holder) inside a glove box. Then the holder 
was sealed in a home-built air-tight bag filled with dry argon and transferred to the 
TEM. The total time of exposure to the air was less than 2 s, which limited the extent 
of Na2O formation on the surface of the Na metal. Prior to the experiment, high-purity 
O2 (99.99%) was introduced to the specimen chamber with a pressure of 1.0 mbar. 
The Au/MnO2 nanowire was manipulated to approach the Na2O layer, and then a 
potential was applied to the nanowire versus the Na metal electrode to either charge or 
discharge the nanowire. Real-time visualization of the structural and phase changes of 
the Au/MnO2 nanowires can be correlated directly to the electrochemical reactions 
based on the above configuration. Due to no elongations for the MnO2 nanowire (NW) 
during ORR, the volume expansion rate in this work was calculated via the increasing 
of the diameter of the NW using the following equation:
