In-situ imaging electrocatalysis in a K-O2 battery with hollandite 
α-MnO2 nanowires air cathode 
Yushu Tang,a Liqiang Zhang,a Yongfu Tang,b Xin Wang,a Teng Zhang,a Rui Yang,a Chi Ma,a Na Li,a 
Yuening Liu,a Xinxin Zhao,a Xionghu Zhang,a Zaifa Wang,b Baiyu Guo,b Yongfeng Li*a and Jianyu 
Huang*b,c
Using α-manganese dioxide (α-MnO2) nanowires as the air 
electrode, a K-O2 nanobattery is assembled in aberration corrected 
environmental transmission electron microscope. It is found that 
the α-MnO2 nanowire is reduced into Mn3O4 and MnO during 
discharge, meanwhile, KO2 is formed on the surface of α-MnO2 
nanowires.
Metal air batteries have attracted worldwide attentions due to 
their much higher theoretical energy density than that of metal 
ion batteries.1–10 Among all kinds of metal air batteries, 
potassium-oxygen (K-O2) battery attracts much attention due to 
its high theoretical energy density (935 Wh/kg), good energy 
efficiency (>90%) and potentially low cost resulting from the 
earth-abundant potassium.11 K-O2 batteries are based on 
reversible one-electron oxygen/potassium superoxide (O2/KO2) 
redox couples, which are introduced by Wu’s group in 2013.12 
The oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) product of K-O2 battery is 
KO2, which is both thermodynamically and kinetically more 
stable than the other superoxides like lithium superoxide (LiO2) 
and sodium superoxide (NaO2).2,12,13
The growth mechanism of KO2 is the key in developing the 
K-O2 battery, which has been studied by several groups.1,3 Lu’s 
group develops a universal KO2 growth model to find that the 
ideal solvent for K-O2 batteries should strongly stabilize 
superoxide to obtain high electrode kinetics and reversibility 
while providing fast oxygen diffusion to achieve high discharge 
capacity.3 It shows that the diffusion rate of oxygen and K+ in 
the K-O2 batteries highly affect the morphology of the KO2. But 
there has been no detailed investigation of the formation 
process of KO2, which is quite significant to understand the 
mechanism of K-O2 battery. The advent of aberration corrected 
environmental transmission electron microscope (AC-ETEM) 
makes it possible to clarify the nucleation and growth 
mechanism of KO2. This kind of technique has been applied to 
reveal the reaction mechanisms of Li-O214,15 and Na-O215–17 
batteries, however no in-situ TEM study has been reported in K-
O2 battery.
In this work, an in-situ metal oxygen battery system was set 
up in an AC-ETEM to investigate the ORR process of K-O2 
battery. Single crystalline α-manganese dioxide (α-MnO2) 
nanowires were used as the cathode.18,19 We investigated the 
nucleation and growth of KO2 as well as the structural changes 
of α-MnO2 upon discharging process. This work provides a 
better understanding of the underlying catalyst mechanism in 
α-MnO2 as well as insight for the design and development of K-
O2 battery. 
α-MnO2 nanowires were synthesized by a hydrothermal 
method (see experimental details in Supplementary data).20 
The transmission electron microscope (TEM) and high-
resolution TEM (HRTEM) images in Fig. S1a,b show that the 
morphology of the nanowire and good crystallinity. X-ray 
diffraction (XRD) results (Fig. S1c) indicate that the pristine α-
MnO2 nanowire is hollandite α-MnO2 (JCPDS card No. 44-0141). 
Due to the use of KMnO4 as the precursor, the K+ ion would be 
expected to occupy the 2a Wyckoff position in the hollandite α-
MnO2 to stabilize its large 2×2 tunnels.18 The energy-dispersive 
X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) results in Figure S2 show that the K 
content in the as-prepared α-MnO2 nanowire is about 4.1 at%. 
During discharge, the hollandite α-MnO2 with axial 2×2 and 1×1 
atomic tunnel offered fast K+ transportation pathways. The 
small radial tunnels offered the radial K+ ion transportation 
channels between the surface and interior of the nanowires.21

nanowires were used as the working cathode and metal K was 
used as the counter electrode. Both of the electrodes were

The morphological evolution of α-MnO2 cathode and the 
formation of KO2 were monitored by a series of time-resolved 
TEM bright field images as shown in Fig. 1b-g and Movie S1. In 
order to minimize the effect of the electron beam on the

condition unless an image needed to be acquired. The α-MnO2 
nanowire undergoes conversion reactions and ORR during the 
potassiation process, triggering two consecutive reaction fronts 
(RFs, shown by blue and red arrowheads in Fig. 1b-g). When a 
negative potential (–3.0 V) was applied to the α-MnO2 nanowire 
against the K counter electrode, the first RF appeared (blue 
arrowheads in Fig. 1c-g). Once the first RF passed by, the 
nanowire showed a slightly expand radially from 59.2 to 64.5 
nm. The reacted nanowire can be bent due to the movement of 
K tip (Fig. 1c), indicating that the toughness of the nanowires is

nanowire, in the low-loss spectrum from position 5 exhibits

In previously study, we have found that the ORR did not 
occur in a Na-O2 battery by using bare α-MnO2 as the air 
cathode.31 However, after coating with Au nanoparticles on the 
α-MnO2 nanowire, plenty of bubble-like NaO2 are formed on 
the 
surface 
of 
the 
nanowire, 
which 
then 
quickly 
disproportionate into Na2O2 and O2 during discharge. For the K-
O2 battery, there are two main differences compared with Na-
O2 batteries. Firstly, the ORR could occur on bare α-MnO2 
nanowire without any noble metals as catalyst because K+ 
preferentially forms thermodynamically stable KO2 due to the 
steric repulsion between cations in the crystal packing 
structure.2 And the produced KO2 is more stable than NaO2 as 
KO2 does not decompose (E0(KO2) = 2.48 V versus E0(K2O2) = 
2.20 V).12 Indeed, KO2 is both thermodynamically and kinetically 
stable.2,12,13 But NaO2 is not thermodynamically favored 
comparing to Na2O2.7 So NaO2 is prone to decompose into 
Na2O2 spontaneously (E0(NaO2) = 2.27 V versus E0(Na2O2) = 2.33 
V),7 especially in the low O2 pressure condition in ETEM (P(O2) = 
1.0 mbar).32 Secondly, KO2 prefers to accumulate on the front 
end of the α-MnO2 nanowire, causing the cracking of the 
nanowire, which suggest the difficult of K+ transport. This may 
be caused by the radius of K+ is larger than that of Na+.
Based on the above discussion, we propose a reaction 
mechanism of K-O2 battery as shown in Fig. 4. For the α-MnO2 
nanowire air electrode, K+ crosses over the K2O electrolyte and 
intercalated into the 8h sites of the 2 × 2 tunnels in the 
hollandite α-MnO2. The K+ transports axially along the α-MnO2 
nanowire via the 2 × 2 tunnels to reduce α-MnO2 to Mn3O4. 
With the potassiation goes on, K+ was extracted radially to the 
surface of nanowire, and reacted with the superoxide ions (O2-) 
formed on the surface of the nanowire via one electron ORR, 
leading to the formation of KO2. This process caused the 
nanowire splitting and cracking and finally reduced to MnO. The 
reaction formula during the discharge process can be presented 
as follows:
3MnO2 + 4K+ + 4e- → Mn3O4 + 2K2O
(1)
Mn3O4 + 2K+ + 2e- → 3MnO + K2O
(2)
K+ + e- + O2 → KO2
(3)

In summary, the two-step mechanism of discharge process 
in the K-O2 batteries reveals an apparent α-MnO2 catalyzed ORR 
by in-situ ETEM studies. Firstly, the K+ transported into the α-
MnO2 causing a noticeable volume increase of the α-MnO2 
nanowire, and the α-MnO2 nanowire was reduced to Mn3O4. 
Secondly, K+ transported radially and reduced the nanowire to 
MnO, caused the cracking in the nanowire and reacts with the 
O2 on the surface to generate KO2 through the ORR process. The 
results provide new insight into the superoxide chemistry in the 
K-O2 battery in real time by in-situ electron microscopy.
