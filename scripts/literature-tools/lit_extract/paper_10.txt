Sodium (Na) metal batteries (SMBs) are potential “beyond lithium (Li)” energy storage technology. However,
uncontrollable Na dendrite growth hinders the practical application of SMBs. The dynamics for Na dendrite
plating/stripping are still unclear, which aﬀects the development of a uniform Na deposition and stripping
strategy. Herein, in situ imaging of the dynamics of Na deposition and stripping was conducted using
a nano-electrochemical device in an advanced aberration corrected environmental transmission electron
microscope (ETEM). Dodecahedron shaped Na nanocrystals with {110} exposed surfaces were formed
during plating. During stripping, Na atoms were extracted layer-by-layer (LBL) along the {110} planes,
which switched to the {112} planes once the extraction encountered the corner of the dodecahedra.
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations indicate that the crystallography of Na deposition and
stripping was controlled by a minimum energy path or Wulﬀ's law, which requires high mass ﬂux to
distribute the newly deposited Na. In situ imaging of Na metal deposition and stripping provides new
understanding of the Na dendrite dynamics, which may have important implications to develop
strategies to suppress Na dendrite growth.
Introduction
Sodium (Na) metal anode-based batteries such as all-solid-state
sodium metal batteries (SMBs), Na–sulfur (Na–S) and Na–air
(Na–O2) batteries are potential “beyond lithium” energy storage
systems for electrical vehicle and renewable energy storage
applications.1–11 However, uncontrollable Na dendrite growth
hinders the development of these battery systems.12–18 It is
suggested that Na dendrite growth is not a replication or
analogue of lithium (Li) dendrite growth.12 For example, Na
dendrites and related anode–electrolyte interfaces are more
sensitive to the electrolyte and external environments in
comparison with Li dendrites. It was observed that in a quasi-
zero electric eld, Na dendrites were gradually dissolved in
a standard battery organic electrolyte (ethylene carbonate (EC)/
diethyl carbonate (DEC)) and broken under external stress.
Conversely, under the same conditions, Li dendrites were rela-
tively stable.16 Some signicant diﬀerences exist between Na
and Li: for example, the radius of Na+ and Li+ ions is 1.02 ˚A and
0.76 ˚A,19 respectively, meaning that Na+ ions may exhibit slower
kinetics than Li+ ions. Furthermore, the melting temperature
(Tm) of Na and Li is 97.72 C and 180.54 C (corresponding to
371 K and 454 K, respectively),12 respectively, suggesting that Na
is more susceptible to creep than Li. The yield strengths of bulk
Na and Li, however, do not exhibit a signicant diﬀerence, i.e.
both are about 1 MPa.20 The yield strength of Na dendrites is
also comparable to that of Li dendrites, i.e. it ranges from tens
of MPa to above 200 MPa, which is much larger than that of
their bulk counterparts.21 Another diﬀerence between Na and Li
is that dendrite growth for Na is considered to occur at the base
(“root”) rather than at the tip.12 Yuhki et al. argued that needle-
like Na dendrites were formed by the granular nucleation of Na
at a pit on a Na-metal electrode and the continuous linear
growth of the deposited Na.22 Although there are extensive
studies on the physics, chemistry, mechanics and electro-

Imaging such dendrites is essential to understand their
growth and to develop mechanisms to prevent their forma-
tion.21,22,25–29 The Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) technique
was used to image Na dendrite growth in b00-Al2O3, and it
revealed a spalling dendritic morphology and the growth of Na
dendrites trailing the crack propagation.25 Hu's group pre-
sented optical microscopy observations of Na dendrite propa-
gation in NASICON ceramic, showing the web-like structure of
Na metal inside the electrolyte.29 Rodrigo et al. reported that Na
deposition in EC, DEC, and propylene carbonate (PC) generated
large volumes of gas and fragile porous dendrites at a symmet-
rical Na/Na optical cell. Fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) greatly
reduced gassing during deposition, and demonstrated superior
cycling performance, impedance, and cycling eﬃciency.26 An
environmental transmission electron microscopy-atomic force
microscopy (ETEM-AFM) platform was used to characterize the
Na/Li dendrite growth in real time with concurrent mechanical
property measurements, revealing that the yield strength of the
electrochemically plated nanocrystalline Na/Li dendrites is over
two orders of magnitude higher than that of bulk Na/Li
metal.21,27,28

Results and discussion
Our experiments were conducted in an ETEM equipped with
a special TEM sample holder that integrates a scanning
tunneling microscopy (STM) probe (TEM-STM, PicoFemto
TEM electrical holder from ZepTools Technology Co., Ltd),
permitting construction of a nanobattery inside the ETEM. The
Na deposition/stripping experiments were conducted in a CO2
atmosphere with 1 mbar pressure (Fig. S1a–c†). The nano-
battery consists of an arc discharged multiwall carbon nano-
tube (MWCNT) attached to a conductive aluminum (Al) rod as
the cathode, Na metal scratched on a sharp Al tip as the counter
electrode, and a naturally grown Na2CO3 layer on the surface of
Na metal as the solid electrolyte. Na2CO3 has been conrmed to
be an ionic conductor and poor electronic conductor, and acts
as a conductor of Na+ ions during deposition and stripping.21
Meantime, the surface Na2CO3 layer plays a critical role in
stabilizing the Na source, thus enabling repeatable real time
deposition and stripping experiments (Fig. S2†). Once a contact
between a MWCNT and the Na2CO3 layer was established, a bias
was applied to the MWCNT against Na metal to stimulate the
plating/stripping processes (Fig. S1d–h and Movie S1†).
Upon applying a negative bias to the MWCNT against the Na
electrode, the MWCNT swelled due to the intercalation of Na
into the MWCNT and the formation of a solid electrolyte
interface (SEI) layer on the surface of the MWCNT. The electron
diﬀraction pattern (EDP) (Fig. S3†) indicates that the SEI layer is
Na2CO3, which was attributed to an electrochemical reaction:
2Na+ + 2CO2 + 2e / Na2CO3 + CO. With sodiation of the
MWCNT, a polyhedron emerged at the triple point of the
MWCNT, Na2CO3 and CO2, and then grew over time (Fig. 1a–f
and Movie S2†). EDP and electron energy loss spectroscopy
(EELS) analyses indicate that the plated Na metal was covered by
a Na2CO3 surface layer (Fig. S1i–k†). The polyhedral Na exhibits
a hexagonal shape with angles between edges being 120, 122
and 118, which are close to the ideal angle between the crys-
talline planes of 120 (Fig. 1f). This is consistent with the law of
conservation of face angles for crystal growth. To determine
accurately the three-dimensional shape of the Na polyhedron,
we tilted the Na crystals to diﬀerent orientations and obtained
the corresponding EDPs (Fig. S4†). A typical EDP of a Na crystal
features a single crystal characteristic, which can be indexed to
single crystalline metallic Na with a [111] zone axis (Fig. 1g). By
comparing the EDP (Fig. 1g) with the corresponding TEM
morphology (Fig. 1f), all the exposed planes are determined to
belong to the {110} family of body-centered-cubic (bcc) Na
(Fig. 1f). High resolution transmission electron microscopy
(HRTEM) analysis of the deposited Na polyhedron conrms that
the deposition plane is (101) (Fig. S5†). Another dodecahedron-
shaped Na crystal formed via a similar Na plating process is
shown in Fig. S6a–f and Movie S3.† TEM images obtained by
sequential tilting of the stripped Na crystal from 10.3 to 25.7

The growth of the Na polyhedron appeared to be isotropic, with

bottom of the polyhedron, where the ion transport path was the
shortest. With progressive stripping, Na+ has to travel a longer
distance to reach the bulk Na at the bottom, therefore the
stripping is slowed down progressively, and eventually the
stripping ceases. In other words, case 2 is an Na+ ion transport
limited process. Thus, these two extreme cases illustrate
elegantly the consequences of electronic and ionic limited
processes on Na stripping behavior.
In brief, where and how the stripping proceeds are deter-
mined by the interplay between ionic and electronic conduc-
tivity in a specic situation. Only when both the ionic
conduction and electronic conduction are good can Na be fully
extracted (almost empty). The diﬀerence between ion conduc-
tion and electron conduction in diﬀerent series of experiments
may be related to the contact between the MWCNT and the
Na2CO3 shell.

The above observations and calculations clearly show that
both the Na deposition and stripping processes follow the
minimum energy paths, and the shape of the Na particle can be
predicted by Wulﬀ's law. In a previous study,21 we have reported
the isotropic growth of Na polyhedral nanocrystals under a low
applied voltage (1.0 or 0.5 V) and the anisotropic growth of
Na nanorods under a high applied voltage (5.0 or 3.5 V) as
illustrated in Fig. 5a. Similar behavior of Li deposition was
observed as well33 although the threshold voltage (from
approximately 0.3 to 0.8 V) is lower than that for Na depo-
sition. The question then arises as to what causes the growth
morphology diﬀerence? It is known that maintaining Wulﬀ's
law requires suﬃcient mass ux (quick mass diﬀusion) inside
the crystal, so it is important to estimate the mass uxes in bulk
Na and Li, respectively. According to our DFT calculations
(Section 4 of the Experimental and computational details), the
formation energies of vacancies are smaller than those of ada-
toms in both bulk Na and Li, indicating that the mass ux in
both bulk Na and Li is dominated by vacancy diﬀusion. The
mass uxes in Na and Li bulk materials at room temperature
were estimated to be 1.99  104 and 4.63  102 ˚A2 s1,
respectively, showing that the mass ux in bulk Na is about two
orders of magnitude larger than that in Li. We present a sche-
matic illustration of the interplay between the metal deposition
rate, which is controlled by the voltage, and mass ux in
determining the Li and Na crystal shape (Fig. 5b). From this
schematic diagram, we can clearly see that at a very low depo-
sition rate or if a low voltage is applied, Wulﬀ's law will domi-
nate the evolution of Na and Li nanocrystals during both
deposition and stripping processes. At a very high deposition
rate or when a high voltage is applied, both deposited Na and Li
grow in a rod-like shape because the deposition rate over-
whelms the mass ux under which Wulﬀ's law is violated, so the
growth is not controlled by the Wulﬀconstruction. When the
voltage or deposition rate is in an intermediate range (black
horizontal line in Fig. 5b), Li whiskers are formed due to their
low mass ux (Fig. 5c1) but the deposited Na will be in the most
stable conguration and its shape is determined by Wulﬀ's law
(Fig. 5c3); a transitional shape of the metal nanocrystals is
predicted when the mass ux is moderate (Fig. 5c2).

formation of this surface layer is very similar to the SEI layer

conrming that our study is instructive for achieving uniform
sodium deposition in anodes.
Regarding the experimental conguration, it is actually
closely related to solid-state batteries (SSBs), in which Li/Na
deposition usually initiates from an individual single point at
the interface between the anode and the solid electrolyte, where
the electrical eld is the highest.35 It is this non-uniform contact
at the interface that causes dendrite growth and failure of SSBs.
Therefore, our work has important implications for real battery
systems.
Conclusion
In summary, in situ imaging of the dynamics of Na deposition
and stripping was conducted using a nano-electrochemical
device in an advanced aberration corrected ETEM. We show
that dodecahedra shaped Na dendrites with {110} exposed
surfaces were formed during the plating process. During strip-
ping, Na atoms were extracted layer-by-layer (LBL) along the
{110} planes rst, and then switched to the {112} planes once
the extraction front encountered a corner of the dodecahedron.
DFT calculations indicate that the Na deposition and stripping
processes follow the minimum energy paths. The shape of the
Na deposition is controlled by the interplay between the atom
deposition rate and mass ux. These results provide new
understanding of the Na deposition/stripping dynamics, which
may oﬀer clues to mitigate Na dendrite growth in SMBs.

The MWCNT used in this experiment were prepared by an

were set to be 0.01 eV ˚A1 and 1.0  106 eV, respectively. To
avoid the interactions between two neighboring images,
a vacuum layer with a thickness greater than 15 ˚A was con-
