Air-Stable Lithium Spheres Produced by Electrochemical Plating
Tingting Yang+, Peng Jia+, Qiunan Liu+, Liqiang Zhang,* Congcong Du+, Jingzhao Chen,
Hongjun Ye, Xiaomei Li, Yanshuai Li, Tongde Shen,* Yongfu Tang,* and Jianyu Huang*
Abstract: Lithium metal is an ideal anode for next-generation
lithium batteries owing to its very high theoretical specific
capacity of 3860 mAhg@1 but very reactive upon exposure to
ambient air, rendering it difficult to handle and transport. Air-
stable lithium spheres (ASLSs) were produced by electro-
chemical plating under CO2 atmosphere inside an advanced
aberration-corrected
environmental
transmission
electron
microscope. The ASLSs exhibit a core–shell structure with
a Li core and a Li2CO3 shell. In ambient air, the ASLSs do not
react with moisture and maintain their core–shell structures.
Furthermore, the ASLSs can be used as anodes in lithium-ion
batteries, and they exhibit similar electrochemical behavior to
metallic Li, indicating that the surface Li2CO3 layer is a good
Li+ ion conductor. The air stability of the ASLSs is attributed
to the surface Li2CO3 layer, which is barely soluble in water
and does not react with oxygen and nitrogen in air at room
temperature, thus passivating the Li core.
Lithium (Li) metal has a very high theoretical specific
capacity of 3860 mAhg@1,[1] making it an attractive anode
material for next-generation high-energy-density batteries for
electric vehicles and grid storage applications. Compared with
Li foil, compacted Li powder based anodes for lithium-ion
batteries can efficiently reduce the formation of Li dendrites
owing to its high surface area, reducing the true current
density during charge/discharge.[2] However, pure Li powders
are unstable and unsafe upon exposure to electrolyte or air as
Li easily reacts with the electrolyte or air to form a solid
electrolyte interface (SEI) or lithium hydroxide (LiOH) and
lithium oxide (Li2O), which causes serious safety problems
during transportation and application. Therefore, Li batteries
are usually assembled in a glove box under a high-purity inert
gaseous atmosphere, which is costly and difficult to handle.
Producing a stable Li powder electrode material that is safe
and easy to handle is important to improve the safety and
efficiency of Li metal batteries and reduce the costs for
handling and processing. As such, numerous efforts have been
focused on stabilizing Li electrodes through controlling the
electrode geometry or electrode surface by using various
additives such as CO2,[3] HF,[4] iodide,[5] benzene,[6] organic
compounds,[7] or wax.[8] However, the synthesis of air-stable Li
powders or spheres by electrochemical plating has not been
reported to date. In a proof-of-concept study, we herein
demonstrate the production of air-stable Li spheres (ASLSs)
using electrochemical plating under a CO2 atmosphere in an
advanced aberration-corrected environmental transmission
electron microscope (ETEM, FEI Titan G2).
The experiments were conducted in CO2 atmosphere at
1.0 mbar pressure in the ETEM (see the Supporting Infor-
mation for details). Upon applying a negative potential to the
carbon nanotube (CNT) against Li in the CO2 atmosphere,
nanospheres with sizes of a few hundred nanometers emerged
at the CNT, Li2O, and CO2 triple point (Figures 1a,b and S1,
Movie S1). The spheres continuously grew larger with time
while maintaining their nearly spherical geometry with some
small indents on the surface (Figure 1b–e). Meanwhile, a thin
shell with dark contrast formed on the surface of the spheres.
After discharging for 2759 s, the spheres exhibited a core–
shell structure, with a core diameter of 996 nm and a shell
thickness of 71 nm (Figure 1e).
The crystalline structure and composition of the nano-
spheres (Figure 1c–e, Figure 2a) were analyzed by electron
diffraction pattern (EDP) analysis and electron energy loss
spectroscopy (EELS). The EDP of the as-formed nano-
spheres exhibits sharp diffraction spots superimposed on
diffraction rings, which are indexed as Li and Li2CO3/Li2O
(Figures 2b and S1), respectively. A dark-field image (Fig-
ure 2c) formed by selecting one of the sharp diffraction spots
from metallic Li (green arrowheads in Figure 2b) indicates
that the core is body-centered-cubic Li, and the shell is
Li2CO3 and Li2O.

ball (Figure 2a) shows three multiple plasmon peaks at 7.5,

To test whether the as-formed spheres were stable in
ambient air, the TEM sample holder with the spheres in it was
taken out of the TEM column and exposed to ambient air
with a humidity of about 30% at room temperature for
30 min (Movie S1). The sample holder was then reinserted
back into the TEM column to identify any structural changes
of the spheres. Surprisingly, the sphere (Figure 2d,f) main-
tained a similar morphology to the as-formed sphere (Fig-
ure 2a,c) after 30 min exposure iro ambient air. The EDP
(Figure 2e) and EELS (Figure 2h,j) from the air-exposed
sphere indicate that both the Li core and the Li2CO3/Li2O
shell remained essentially unchanged after air exposure,
confirming that the Li spheres were air-stable.
To confirm the repeatability of the experiment, we
synthesized another sphere (Figure 3a–c), and then exposed
it to ambient air for 30 min; its morphology is presented in
Figure 3d,f. The air-exposed sphere (Figure 3d,f) had a sim-
ilar shape to that of the as-formed ball (Figure 3c,e) except
that a ribbon-like nanoobject appeared on the surface of the
former, the reason for which is unclear. Dark-field images of
the spheres before (Figure 3e) and after (Figure 3 f) air
exposure exhibit similar core–shell structure, with the core
being Li and the shell being Li2CO3/Li2O. The EDPs from the
spheres before (Figure 3g) and after (Figure 3h) air exposure
are similar, showing diffraction rings superimposed on sharp
diffraction spots, which are indexed as Li2CO3/Li2O and Li,
respectively. The similarities of the crystalline structure and
morphology between the pristine and air-exposed spheres
indicate that the Li spheres with a surface shell are air-stable.
We thus refer to them as ASLSs. To test their air stability
further, the as-formed ASLSs were exposed to air with much
higher humidity of 45% and 70%, respectively. We found that
the ASLSs were quite stable in a humidity of 45% (Fig-
ure S3). However, when the humidity reached 70%, some of
the ASLSs corroded (Figure S4). The stability of the ASLSs

can protect Li metal from further oxidation. Similar anti-

Moreover, Li2CO3 is only slightly soluble in water,[10] which
further confirms its excellent anticorrosion abilities in air. We
also generated Li/Li2O core–shell spheres by electrochemical
plating in O2 atmosphere, and we found that these Li/Li2O
spheres were not air-stable (Figure S5 and Movie S2). After
exposure to ambient air, the contrast of the sphere changed
from the original uniform gray (Figure S5a) to heavily
strained (Figure S5b), and the EDPs indicate that the as-
formed Li/Li2O (Figure S5c) was converted into LiOH
(Figure S5d) upon air exposure. These results constitute
clear evidence that the air stability of the Li/Li2CO3/Li2O
spheres can be ascribed to the formation of Li2CO3 rather
than Li2O.
It is interesting to note that Li does not chemically react
with CO2 in the absence of water at room temperature, which
is in contrast to our observation that Li reacts with CO2
electrochemically at room temperature.[12] This indicates that
there is an energy barrier for the reaction between Li and
CO2, and a driving force is required to overcome the energy
barrier so that the reaction can take place.
To test whether the air-exposed ASLSs can be used as an
anode for Li-ion batteries, we constructed a nanobattery using
an air-exposed ASLS as the anode, the Li2CO3/Li2O shell as
the electrolyte, and an Au-coated CuO nanowire as the
cathode inside the ETEM (Figures 4 and S6, Movie S3). Upon
applying a negative potential to the CuO nanowire against the
ASLS, the CuO nanowire was quickly lithiated (Figure 4), as
evidenced by its volume expansion and the formation of Li2O,
Cu, and LiAu, which is similar to the lithiation of the CuO
nanowire observed using fresh metallic Li as the anode.[13] The
result agrees very well with a literature report that Li2CO3 is
a Li ion conductor with a conductivity of the same order to
that of other Li ion conductors, such as Li4SiO4, Li5AlO4, and
Li2SO4.[14]

Electrochemical testing indicated that the ASLS electrode
showed a much higher first cycle capacity than a bare Li
electrode (Figure S8h). This result confirms that ASLSs are
a versatile anode material for lithium batteries. The structure
of the ASLSs is similar to the surface-stabilized Li powders
produced by the droplet emulsion technique (DET), which
also exhibit a Li2CO3 shell and a Li core.[15] The stabilized Li
powders were compacted and used as the electrode for Li-ion
battery,[15] and results show that dendrite growth in the
compacted electrode was reduced significantly as compared
with an extruded Li foil electrode. In a related study, it was
reported that Li2CO3 particles treated graphite electrode
showed much better capacity retention, particularly for the
first cycle, as compared to the non-treated electrode, because
the SEI layer with a higher Li2CO3 concentration enhanced
the stability and assisted Li+ diffusion, improving the capacity
retention.[16] Remarkably, Asadi and co-workers recently
reported a record-long cycle lifetime of 700 cycles for a Li-
air battery by using a Li2CO3-protected Li foil as the anode
and cycling in a simulated air atmosphere. They attributed the
record-long cycle lifetime to the presence of a Li2CO3
passivation layer on the Li surface, which protects the
metallic Li from corrosion by the moisture, CO2, and O2 in
an air-like atmosphere.[17] In addition, the Li2CO3 coating on
the graphite surface can prevent mechanical damage occur-
ring at the electrode surfaces, which causes a drastic capacity
loss in the first cycle in Li-ion batteries, a phenomenon that is
referred to as electrochemical shock.[16,18] In another relevant
study, stabilized Li electrodes showed better first-cycle
efficiencies than a pure Li electrode,[19] indicating the great
application potential of stabilized Li powders in next-
generation Li batteries and Li-ion batteries. In a similar
vein, we expect the ASLSs to be safe and efficient anode
materials for next-generation lithium batteries. The electro-
chemical plating method described here provides an efficient
strategy for the preparation of ASLSs with reactivity similar
to that of conventional Li anodes.
