Mechanistic Understanding of the Underlying Energy
Storage Mechanism of 𝜶-MnO2-based
Pseudo-Supercapacitors
Lei Deng, Zaifa Wang, Hantao Cui, Yunna Guo, Zhangran Ye, Hui Li, Xinyu Zhang,
Peng Jia,* Qiaobao Zhang,* and Liqiang Zhang*
Manganese dioxide (𝜶-MnO2) has attracted signiﬁcant research interest
in supercapacitors recently. However, the reaction mechanism of 𝜶-MnO2
in supercapacitors remains unclear. Therefore, a nano-supercapacitor using
Environmental transmission electron microscopy (ETEM) is conducted and
investigated the reaction mechanism of 𝜶-MnO2 based on three ionic liquids
(ILs). It found that in the aprotic ionic liquid (AIL) 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium
triﬂuoromethanesulfonate (EMIMOTF), 𝜶-MnO2 nanowires (NWs)
undergo an oxidation reaction due to the presence of an active proton at the
second position (H2) of the imidazole ring. As a result, 𝜶-MnO2 NWs undergo
a phase transition and transform into Mn3O4, exhibiting pseudo-capacitive
properties. Furthermore, characterization of the macroscopic 𝜶-MnO2
electrodes after cycling reveals that after the initial charging cycles, the
dominant energy storage mechanism of the supercapacitor transitions from
pseudo-capacitance to a dual-layer capacitance formed by the combination
of Mn3O4 and unreacted 𝜶-MnO2. Simultaneously, due to the coexistence
of these two energy storage mechanisms, the speciﬁc capacitance of the
supercapacitor in EMIMOTF electrolyte reaches up to 80 F g−1, and the cycle
number reaches as high as 1000 cycles. The results are expected to provide
insights into the selection of electrolytes in supercapacitors and oﬀer a
fundamental understanding of the internal reaction mechanisms in capacitors.

1. Introduction
Sustainable
and
renewable
energy
re-
sources
are
being
intensively
pursued
owing to the diminishing supply of fossil
fuels and global warming. Manganese
dioxide (𝛼-MnO2) based supercapacitors
have relatively high energy densities and
a longer cycle life than those of tradi-
tional batteries.[1–3]
It utilized 𝛼-MnO2
as the active material for energy storage.
The 𝛼-MnO2 electrode exhibited pseudo-
capacitive behavior, enabling high energy
storage capacity and fast charge-discharge
rates.[4,5]
However,
𝛼-MnO2
based
su-
percapacitors still have several technical
bottlenecks:
1)
Limited
cyclability
and
stability;[6,7] 2) The unclear microscopic
mechanism. Therefore, the development
of 𝛼-MnO2 based supercapacitors is still a
research hotspot.[8–10]
In 𝛼-MnO2-based material supercapaci-
tors, the electrolyte is a key part that governs
the overall performance.[11–15] Ye et al.[16]
revealed the deposition/dissolution process
in aqueous electrolyte with diﬀerent pro-
ton concentrations, ﬁnding that MnOOH
is the Mn3+-intermediate in neutral electrolytes and then chem-
ically dissolved in the electrolyte as the proton concentration in-
creases. The dissolution causes a loss of active species and greatly
decreases the discharge capacity. Common aqueous electrolytes,
due to the water decomposition at high voltages, have narrow po-
tential windows (0–1.0 V), which limit the capacitance.[17–19] To
solve this problem, ionic liquid (IL) families might be promising
electrolytes to increase the operating voltage for supercapacitors,
which have low vapor pressure, large electrochemical potential
windows (0–3.0 V), and good thermal and chemical stability.[20]
Shi et al.[21] showed the electrochemical behavior in a series of
ILs, ﬁnding that graphene sheet ionic liquid exhibits the highest
speciﬁc capacitance due to the cation and anion insertion. Chang
et al.[22] indicate that the linear-shape anion in ILs, such as di-
cyanamide (DCA−) and thiocyanate (SCN−) anions, instead of the
cation with a large imidazolium ring, is the working ion that can
be reversibly inserted into and deserted from the tunnels between
the [MnO6] octahedral units. The previous reports only focused
on the electrochemical performance of supercapacitors and there
is no study on the mechanism of the faradaic process between

2. Results and Discussion
2.1. Nano-Supercapacitor
To validate the faradaic process in 𝛼-MnO2 based capacitors, an
in situ study was conducted on a pseudo-supercapacitor based
on 𝛼-MnO2 using the ETEM (FEI, Titan G2, 300 kV). The exper-
imental setup is illustrated in Figure S1 (Supporting Informa-
tion). The nano-supercapacitor comprises an anode composed
of 𝛼-MnO2 NWs attached to a conductive aluminum (Al) rod,
and the counter electrode consists of ILs scratched on a sharp
Al tip in conjunction with 𝛼-MnO2 NWs. ILs serve as the con-
ductor for 1-ethylimidazole, 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium, and
hydrogen cation ions (EIM+, EMIM+, and H+ ions) during the
discharge process. Moreover, it is well known that ionic liquids
are stable under the electron beam to prevent decomposition or
evaporation. Once a contact angle between 𝛼-MnO2 NWs and ILs
was established, a negative bias was applied to the 𝛼-MnO2 NWs
against the counter electrode to stimulate the faradaic process
in supercapacitor storing charges. Figure S2 (Supporting Infor-
mation) presents the hydrothermal synthesis of 𝛼-MnO2 pow-
der, and the characterization of the sample is shown in Figure
S3 (Supporting Information). The tetragonal 𝛼-MnO2 NWs (PDF
44–0141) are characterized by a high-resolution transmission
electron microscope (HRTEM) image and the corresponding se-
lected area electron diﬀraction (SAED) pattern can be observed
in Figure S3b,c (Supporting Information). The SAED of the pris-
tine 𝛼-MnO2 NWs can be indexed as (101), (3̄10) and (21̄1) planes
of 𝛼-MnO2. Moreover, the 𝛼-MnO2 NWs are characterized by the
high-angle annular dark ﬁeld (HAADF, Figure S3d–g, Support-
ing Information) images indicating the pristine 𝛼-MnO2 NWs
along [001] zone axis. The 𝛼-MnO2 NWs have an irregular sur-
face with several facets and possess ideal 1 × 1 (1.9 Å × 1.9 Å) and
2 × 2 (4.6 Å × 4.6 Å) atomic tunnels which match well with the
atomic model of 𝛼-MnO2. Not only that there are some 2 × 4 and 4
× 4 tunnels, formation of intermediate tunnels, in high magniﬁ-
cation shown in Figure S3g (Supporting Information).[23] Due to
the use of KMnO4 as the precursor, the structure of 𝛼-MnO2 stabi-
lized by K+ introduced during the synthesis process. The HAADF
images provide a clear visualization of the bright spots surround-
ing each tunnel, which correspond to the Mn atomic columns
(indicated by the purple dots).
Figure S4 (Supporting Information) shows the proton nu-
clear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H NMR) of ILs. It is
known to all that the key feature that distinguishes protic ionic
liquids (PILs) from other ILs is the proton transfer from the
acid to the base. Due to the incomplete proton transfer, there
are lots of neutral species and ion pair aggregates.[24,25] As for
the PIL 1-ethylimidazolium hydrogen sulfate (EIMHSO4), the
highest positive charge is concentrated on the proton at po-
sition 2 on the imidazole ring (H2), and the respective peak
is shifted downﬁeld signiﬁcantly (9.17 ppm). It is worth not-
ing that 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium triﬂuoromethanesulfonate
(EMIMOTF) is typically classiﬁed as an aprotic ionic liquid (AIL).
However, an interesting observation is that the chemical shift
of the proton H2 occurring at 8.78 ppm suggests a diﬀerent
chemical environment or interaction compared to what was pre-
viously mentioned.[26,27] And the 1-butyl-1-methylpyrrolidinium
bis(triﬂuoromethanesulfonyl) imide (PYR14TFSI) is an AIL with
no active protons. The general trend observed in this series of
spectra is that as the strength of the acid increases, the chemi-
cal shift of the exchangeable proton is shifted upﬁeld (to lower
ppm values). Moreover, Gehrke et al.[25] indicated that hydrogen
bonds of the imidazolium cation are primarily formed by the
most acidic hydrogen atom, H2, and along with the other hy-
drogen atoms of the cation ring, H4, and H5 (protons at posi-
tions 4 and 5, respectively). Watanable et al.[28] revealed that the
proton conduction mechanism changes from proton hopping to
a vehicle mechanism with increasing acidity in PILs. This sug-
gests that the hydrogen bonding interactions involving H2 can
signiﬁcantly inﬂuence the chemical environment and chemical
shifts in EMIMOTF, which is proven by experimental data in the
following.
To investigate the diﬀerent storage mechanisms of the 𝛼-
MnO2-based supercapacitor involving H2 of diﬀerent chem-
ical environments in EIMHSO4 and EMIMOTF electrolytes,
Figure 1 illustrates the structure and phase characterization of
𝛼-MnO2 NWs during the charging process. The phase structure
of the discharge product was investigated by SAED (Figure 1c–e)
marked by “I”, “II” and “III” respectively. During the discharge
process, the diﬀraction spots (marked by yellow) in the com-
pletely reactive segment (Figure 1e) become diﬀused arching and
can be indexed as (01̄3) and (̄11̄2) planes of tetragonal Mn3O4
(PDF 24–0734), at the same time the diﬀraction spots of unre-
active 𝛼-MnO2 (marked by brown) are dim. Notably the same
as the above experiment phenomenon is observed for AIL EMI-
MOTF. The SAED (Figure 1h–j) shows discharge product is
Mn3O4 indicating the EMIMOTF is associated with a proton con-
duction between the EMIMOTF and 𝛼-MnO2. For PYR14TFSI,
the sharp diﬀraction spots of 𝛼-MnO2 (Figure 1m–o) indicate
there is no faradaic process in AILs with any active proton. The
raw diﬀraction images about relative segments (III parts) in
EIMHSO4 and EMIMOTF are shown in Figure S5 (Supporting
Information).
The above SAED results are supported by electron energy
loss spectroscopy (EELS) of the relative segment (Figure 1p,q),
in which the oxygen pre-peak (O-K), and Mn-L2,3 edge are in-

2.2. Macro-Supercapacitor
To investigate the electrochemical response of 𝛼-MnO2 in ILs and
establish the consistency of 𝛼-MnO2 charge storage at both the
macroscopic and microscopic scales. We conducted the 𝛼-MnO2-
based supercapacitors with diﬀerent ILs, and the chemical perfor-
mance of EMIMOTF is presented in Figure 2a–d. Figure 2a indi-
cates that the cathodic and anodic breakdown potentials of this IL
are ≈−3.0 and 3.0 V, respectively. Besides, we found the 𝛼-MnO2
was unstable in this electrolyte. After a few Cyclic Voltammetry
(CV) scans, an irreversible cathodic reaction occurred which is lo-
cated at 1.7 V. As the scan increases to the third cycle, the cathodic
reaction has almost completely disappeared and the CV curves
exhibit rectangular shapes and mirror-image features.[8,10,31,32] A
similar response is shown in Figure 2b. As can be seen, the
charging and discharging branches are non-linear and asymmet-
ric in the initial cycles, again indicating the irreversible pseudo-
capacitive performance of 𝛼-MnO2 at a potential range of 1.5–
2.0 V. A near triangular-shaped galvanostatic charge–discharge
(GCD) proﬁles after the initial cycles, is indicative of smooth
charge propagation across the two electrodes, characteristics of a
good electrochemical capacitor. Figure S7 (Supporting Informa-
tion) shows the X-ray diﬀraction (XRD) spectrum of both pos-
itive and negative electrodes in EMIMOTF. While there is no
phase transformation at the positive electrode (brown line), the
XRD spectrum indicates the presence of Mn3O4 at the negative
electrode (black line). Overall, the faradaic process involving the
transformation between MnO2 and Mn3O4 occurs during the ini-
tial charges. This has been proven by HAADF images shown
in Figure S8 (Supporting Information). Figure S8a (Supporting
Information) illustrates a phase transformation from MnO2 to
Mn3O4 at the negative electrode, while no phase transformation
occurs at the positive electrode in Figure S8b (Supporting In-
formation). This ﬁnding is consistent with the results presented
above.
Figure 2c displays that the capacitance measured at 0.1–2.0 V
in the ﬁrst cycle exceeds 80 F g−1. It is observed that 𝛼-MnO2
in pure IL has unstable performance with ≈41% retention of
capacity in the ﬁrst ten cycles. This poor cycling performance
is caused by irreversible pseudo-capacitance. Furthermore, there
is 76% capacitance retention after 1000 cycles, due to a contri-
bution from double-layer capacitance of Mn3O4. The GCD pro-
ﬁles at diﬀerent current densities of 50, 200, 300, and 500 mA
g−1 are shown in Figure 2d. A good linear relationship of the
charging–discharging potentials with time at the current den-
sities of 300 and 500 mA g−1 is observed, indicating a smooth
charge propagation across the two electrodes, due to a low contri-
bution of pseudo-capacitance. However, the curves display a non-
triangular shape at the low current densities of 50 and 200 mA
g−1, due to an irreversible pseudo-capacitance, resulting a poor
coulombic eﬃciency. A non-triangular-shaped GCD proﬁle, as
observed for the supercapacitor at the current densities of 50
and 200 mA g−1, indicates that most charge propagation across
the two electrodes is not smooth so that characteristics of a poor
coulombic eﬃciency in the ﬁrst forty cycles. Other electrochem-
ical performances are shown in Figure S9 (Supporting Informa-
tion), illustrating the CV proﬁles and the GCD proﬁles of 𝛼-MnO2
electrodes measured in EIMHSO4, EMIMOTF, and PYR14TFSI
respectively.

3. Conclusion
The interaction between 𝛼-MnO2 and three diﬀerent ILs, namely
EIMHSO4, EMIMOTF, and PYR14TFSI, exhibits varying proton
activity. Our ﬁndings indicate that the faradaic process is inﬂu-
enced by the presence of protons at the imidazole ring. Through
in situ ETEM, we observed that the 𝛼-MnO2 electrode displays
signiﬁcant instability when exposed to EIMHSO4, whereas no
phase transformation occurs when exposed to PYR14TFSI. This
observation explains the low cyclic stability and low speciﬁc ca-
pacitance of the supercapacitor in the former case. Notably, in
situ ETEM results also revealed that 𝛼-MnO2 undergoes reduc-
tion to Mn3O4 in the aprotic ionic liquid (EMIMOTF). Further-
more, a considerable number of well-crystallized Mn3O4 par-
ticles were observed in the electrode after extended cycling of
the macroscopic supercapacitor. In summary, EMIMOTF proves
to be a suitable electrolyte for 𝛼-MnO2 based supercapacitors,
which encompass both pseudo-capacitive and double-layer capac-
itive characteristics. These results contribute to a fundamental
understanding of the faradaic mechanism between protons and
