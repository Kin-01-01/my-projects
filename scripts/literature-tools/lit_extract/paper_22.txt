Metal air batteries, in particular Li-air (more precisely Li-O2) batteries, have attracted 
worldwide attention due to their high theoretical energy density for potential applications such as 
grid energy storage and vehicle electrification. The theoretical energy densities are 3500 Wh kg-1 
for Li-O2 batteries and 1600 Wh kg-1 for Na-O2 batteries, which are much higher than that of the 
state-of-the-art Li-ion batteries.1-11 Researches on Li-O2 and Na-O2 batteries quickly evolve into 
Li-O2/CO2 (mixture of O2 and CO2 gases) and Na-O2/CO2 batteries, as it was discovered that 
introducing CO2 into the Li-O2 and Na-O2 systems can significantly influence the electrochemical 
reactions and increase the energy storage capacity.12-15 Interestingly, it was discovered recently 
that CO2 itself can act as a reactant gas to react with Li and Na, thus forming Li-CO2 or Na-CO2 
batteries.12, 13, 16-22 Using CO2 instead of O2 as the cathode is a very promising clean energy storage 
strategy in the context that it provides not only an energy storage device but also an effective 
technology for CO2 capture, reducing greenhouse gas, thus easing the global warming. The Na-
CO2 battery is advantageous over the Li-CO2 battery in the same context as the Na-O2 battery over 
the Li-O2 battery in that Na is earth abundant, relatively easy to extract, and lower cost as compared 
to Li. The Archer group has successfully developed a Na-O2/CO2 battery.12 They reported a high 
specific energy of 6500-7000 Wh kg-1 in a gas mixture of CO2 and O2, which is 2 to 3 times higher 
than that in pure O2. The Na-O2/CO2 “primary” battery was soon advanced into a rechargeable 
secondary battery by the same group.17 But rechargeable Na-CO2 battery has not been achieved 
until recently.22 Hu et al.23 created quasi-solid-state rechargeable Na-CO2 batteries with reduced 
graphene oxide Na anodes. The CO2 batteries were cycled successfully for 400 cycles with a fixed 
capacity of 1000 mAh g-1. Although researches in solid-state metal-O2 and/or CO2 batteries evolve 
very rapidly,24-29 the fundamental electrochemistry and the reaction mechanisms are still under 
debate. For example, what are the discharge products? What is the morphology of the discharge

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Morphology Evolution of the Discharge/Charge Products
A metal-O2/CO2 nanobattery was constructed as shown in Figure 1a. Detailed experimental 
procedure is described in the Method section. Upon applying a negative potential to the carbon 
nanotube (CNT) nano cathode against the counter electrode Na (the Na electrode was grounded in 
our experiment), a ball emerged at the CNT and the surface of Na substrate (Figure S1) contact 
point, and the ball grew with lapse of time (Figure 1b, Movie S1, hereafter the nanobattery with a 
CNT cathode is referred to as Na-O2/CO2/CNT battery: meaning a metal-gas battery with a O2 and 
CO2 mixed gas and CNT as cathode). The balls were generally spherical, although other irregular 
morphology was occasionally observed. All the balls had a core-shell structure with the thin 
surface shell wrapping around the inner core which showed an almost uniform contrast. However, 
tiny speckle-like nano clusters can be seen inside the core (Figure S2a 139 s, marked by red 
circles). At 176 s, the ball grew to 174 nm (Figure 1b). The bias on the CNT was then reversed, 
when the ball started to shrink, which initiated from the thickening of the surface shell (Figure 1c), 
followed by indentation from surface to the core, continued by contracting. At 22 s in Figure 1c, 
grainy porous contracted ball formed, which continued shrinkage until its disappearance. Upon 
applying a negative bias on the CNT vs Na, a new ball grew at the same location where the CNT 
and the Na substrate intersected (Figure 1d, 1f), and the ball shrank again at a reversed bias (Figure 
1e, 1g), and the processes were repeatable for multiple cycles until the CNT was consumed or 
broken. Concurrent with the growth and shrinkage of the ball, the CNT became thicker and thinner,

batteries operating in the real air.43 Another phenomenon was that the end of the CNT where it 
contacted the Na substrate became thinner after each cycle, and the thinning process occurred at 
charging, but not at discharging, indicating that the CNT was consumed during charging.
Structural Characterization by Electron Diffraction Pattern (EDP)
Structural evolution of the balls during growth was monitored by EDP in real time (Figure 2, 
Movies S4-S6). Before the ball emerged, its EDP showed sharp diffraction rings which are indexed 
as body-centered-cubic (BCC) Na (JCPDS card No. 01-0850), hexagonal Na2O2 (JCPDS card No. 
09-0075) and face-centered-cubic (FCC) Na2O (JCPDS card No. 23-0528), respectively (Figure 
2a). With increasing ball size, the EDPs showed scattered diffraction spots and diffused diffraction 
rings superimposed on an amorphous-like diffused scattering background (Figure 2b). These sharp 
diffraction spots are indexed as Na. The diffused diffraction rings are indexed as hexagonal Na2O2 
and Na2CO3 (JCPDS card No. 86-0311). The diffused scattering in the EDPs indicates a poor 
crystallinity and a small grain size of the discharge products (Figure S2a, marked by red circles). 
As the bias was reversed, the ball shrank, and the corresponding EDPs changed from diffused 
diffraction rings to well-defined diffraction rings (Figure 2c, 2d), and most of which are indexed 
as hexagonal Na2CO3, which contains a little Na and Na2O2. The diffractions rings evolved into

Composition Analysis by Electron Energy Loss Spectroscopy (EELS)
The same phenomena were observed in the time-resolved scanning transmission electron 
microscopy - annular dark field (STEM-ADF) images (Figure 3a, 3b, Movie S7), in which the 
samples were illuminated by the electron beam intermittently when taking images, therefore the 
total electron dose experienced by the samples was much less than in the TEM mode. Quantitative 
investigations on how the electron dose was affecting the electrochemical reaction is described in 
the Figure S3. The results indicate that the reactions were electrochemically driven, rather than 
under the influence of the e-beam. The consumption of carbon was observed in the charging 
process, indicating that carbon was involved in the charging reaction. EELS spectra of the 
discharged and charged products are shown in Figures 3c, 3d, respectively. Low-loss EELS from 
the core of both the discharged and charged balls show similar profiles (Figure 3c) with three 
multiple plasmon peaks located at 5.7, 11.5, 17.3 eV and three additional low intensity plasmon 
peaks situated at 22.9, 31.1, 37.6 eV; these peak positions and profile show excellent repeatability. 
Combining with the EDP results, we assign these characteristic peaks to that of metallic Na and 
Na2O2. The shells of both the discharged and charged balls exhibit almost the same profile as 
shown in the green plots in Figure 3c and 3d. The low-loss spectrum of the shell displays a wide 
plasmon peak spanning from 5 to about 30 eV with a peak energy located at 19.6 eV. Some minor 
peaks at 8.6, 13.2, 33.5 eV residing on the rising side of the broadened halo. In the core-loss

Discussion
Based on the EDP and EELS results, the following reaction mechanism is deduced (Figure 
4). When the CNT was negatively biased against the Na counter electrode, the Na+ crossed the 
solid electrolyte, in the meantime, the electrons were transported from the CNT to where it touched 
the Na substrate, as soon as the Na+ and the electrons met at the CNT and Na substrate contact 
point (Figure 4a), the following discharge reaction took place: 
2Na+ + 2e- + O2 → Na2O2      (1)
Followed by a chemical reaction:
2Na2O2 + 2CO2 → 2Na2CO3 + O2      (2)
Concurrently the electrochemical deposition of Na occurred: 
Na+ + e- → Na      (3)
Which enlarged the ball to form a Na and Na2O2 core and a Na2CO3 shell.

When the CNT was positively biased against the Na counter electrode, the electrochemical 
decomposition of the Na2CO3 shell assisted by carbon consumption occurred:
2Na2CO3 + C → 4Na+ + 3CO2 + 4e-      (4)
Concurrently Na striping reaction took place: 
Na → Na+ + e-      (5)
After the outer Na2CO3 was decomposed (reaction (4), hereafter referred to as R-(4)), the 
Na2O2 in the core reacted with CO2 to form Na2CO3 (R-(2)) again. The newly formed Na2CO3 
continued its reaction with CNT and decomposed gradually to form Na and CO2 (R-(4)). The 
consumption of carbon in charging was clearly observed in Figure 3b and Figures S5-S7, in which 
the corrosion and shortening of CNT were present during the charging of the Na-O2/CO2/CNT 
nanobattery. Generally in the metal-air batteries with organic electrolytes, the carbonates as 
discharge products or by-products are difficult to decompose completely due to their poor 
electronic conductivity. A required high overpotential to decompose the carbonates concurrently 
oxidizes the organic electrolyte and releases the

of Na2CO3 might take place via the following reaction:
2Na2CO3 → 4Na+ + 2CO2 +O2 +4e-      (6)
The Gibbs free energy for R-(6) was calculated to be ΔrG = 1306 kJ mol-1 (ΔrG = -zFE0, ΔG 
= -zFE, where z, F and E are molar electrons, Frarady’s constant and the potential, respectively), 
which corresponds to a potential of E0 = -3.38 V versus Na/Na+. While the Gibbs free energy for 
R-(4) was calculated to be ΔrG = 905.6 kJ mol-1, which corresponds to a potential of E0 = -2.35 V 
(Table 1). Therefore thermodynamically R-(4) is much easier to occur than R-(6), highlighting the 
critical role of carbon in facilitating the decomposition of Na2CO3.48 This is further supported by 
our experimental results which showed that in the absence of carbon the decomposition of Na2CO3 
was extremely difficult. We conducted discharge/charge reactions of the Na-O2/CO2 nanobattery 
with an Ag nanowire as the cathode (hereafter referred to as Na-O2/CO2/Ag, Figures 5-6, Movie 
S8). The discharge reaction for the Na-O2/CO2/Ag nanobattery followed similar steps to that of 
the Na-O2/CO2/CNT nanobattery, i.e. it went through R-(1) to R-(3) (Figures 5-6, Movie S8), as 
verified by the EDPs and EELS characterizations of the discharge and charge products (Figure 6). 
However, the charge reaction differed considerably, i.e. the discharge product was very difficult 
to decompose for the Na-O2/CO2/Ag nanobattery, and even after prolonged charging, full 
decomposition of the discharged balls was not achieved (Figure 5, Movie S8). This is in sharp 
contrast to the Na-O2/CO2/CNT nanobattery in which the decomposition of Na2CO3 was facile and 
complete (Figures 1, 2, S5-S7, Movies S1-S7). The experiments underscore again the importance 
of carbon in facilitating the decomposition of Na2CO3.11 Li et al.49 also reported that Li2CO3 itself 
is difficult to decompose, but it can be electrochemically decomposed in the presence of carbon 
above 3.2 V.

To further clarify the electrochemical reaction mechanism of the Na-O2/CO2/CNT battery, 
we also conducted control experiments in the Na-O2/CNT battery and Na-CO2/CNT battery under 
similar experimental conditions to the Na-O2/CO2/CNT battery except for the different 
atmosphere. Figure 7 (Movie S9) and Figure S8 (Movie S10) show the time lapse structure 
evolution of the discharge and charge products of the Na-O2 nanobattery. In Figure 7, when we 
applied a negative potential to the CNT, a tiny ball was formed at the surface of Na substrate, CNT, 
and O2 triple point, and it then grew with lapse of time. When the bias was reversed, the ball shrank 
slightly, but it was difficult to completely decompose the ball even at a very high overpotential. 
The structure and composition of the ball were analyzed by EDP and EELS. As shown in Figure 
7c-d, the discharge products were identified to be Na, Na2O2, and Na2O. After charge, little change 
was observed in the EDP, indicating that the decomposition of Na2O2 was difficult in the absence 
of CO2. In addition, EELS spectra of the discharge and charge products are shown in Figure 7e-f. 
Low-loss EELS spectra from the core of both discharged and charged ball show similar profiles, 
being ascribed to Na and Na2O2. Similar EELS profiles were detected in the shells of both the 
discharged and the charged balls, with three plasma peaks at 5.9, 8.7 and 12.9 eV. In the core loss 
spectra, the O K-edge peak was observed in both the core and shell of the ball, while C was not 
detected in all the products. The EELS spectra confirm that the discharged/charged balls are Na

CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we conducted successfully in-situ discharging/charging of Na-O2/CO2 solid-
state batteries in an advanced aberration corrected ETEM, and the electrochemical reaction 
mechanisms in the nanobatteries were revealed in real time. During discharging, Na2O2 were 
formed via Na+ + e- + O2 → Na2O2 followed by Na2O2 chemically reacting with CO2 to form 
Na2CO3 and O2; during charging, Na2CO3 was reduced by C to form Na and CO2. The CO2 and 
carbon facilitated the electrochemical reactions in the Na-O2/CO2 battery and improved its 
cyclability. Without C, the Na-O2/CO2/Ag nanobattery showed poor reversibility. Without CO2, 
the Na-O2 battery exhibits poor cyclability due to the sluggish decomposition of Na2O2. Our 
studies provide important mechanistic understanding to the electrochemistry in the Na-O2/CO2 and 
Na-O2 batteries, which may aid the design of these batteries for future large energy storage 
applications.

The CNT was synthesized via the arc discharging method60, 61 using a stainless steel discharge 
chamber with a high purity (99.999%) N2, He and Ar atmosphere. The anode was asphalt carbon

controlled within the range of 0.04-0.05 MPa in this experiment. The DC voltage and current of 
the experiment were controlled at 18-20 V and 60-80 A, respectively. During the experiment, the 
distance between the two electrodes was maintained at about 1-3 mm by manually advancing the 
anode that was consumed. 
In-Situ Environmental TEM of the Na-O2/CO2 Nanobattery Construction and Operation 
The experiments were conducted in a FEI Titan G2 ETEM. The solid-state battery was 
constructed based on our previous all solid state battery,62-64 which consists of a Na anode, a Na2O2 
and Na2CO3 electrolyte, and a single nanotube or nanowire as the cathode for the discharge/charge 
reactions. More specifically, a tungsten (W) tip scratched metal sodium (Na) was used as a 
negative electrode, and a carbon nanotube (CNT) or a silver (Ag) nanowire was used as a medium 
for the discharge/charge reactions. A mixed gas with CO2 to O2 ratio of 50:50 or a pure O2 gas 
with a gas pressure of 1 mbar was used during the in-situ experiments. The electrochemical 
reaction was controlled by external biasing using a potentiostat (PiciFemto V3ST, produced by 
Anhui Zeyou Technology Co., Ltd.). When a negative/positive bias was applied to the CNT or Ag 
nanowire against the Na counter electrode, the discharge/charge reactions took place.
