A B S T R A C T  
Iron-based fluorides (IBFs, typically FeF3 and FeF2) are potential next generation high energy density cathodes 
for lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) due to their high specific capacity, cost effectiveness and crustal abundance. 
However, IBFs decompose electrolyte violently which impedes their practical implementation in LIBs. Herein we 
report a strategy to embed FeF2 nanoparticles in an interconnected cyclic polyacrylonitrile (cPAN) network to 
passivate the cathode/electrolyte interface. Moreover, cPAN affords strong adhesion to both the FeF2 nano­
particles and the current collector, essentially acting as a binder that is superior to polyvinylidene fluoride 
(PVDF). Cryo-transmission electron microscopy reveals that the amorphous cPAN was uniformly coated on the 
FeF2 surface with a thickness of 5 nm, acting as an effective (cathode electrolyte interphase) CEI layer that 
inhibited the growth of excessive CEI, thus achieved stable cycling in lean electrolyte. When paired with the high 
capacity SiO/C anode, the FeF2-cPAN|SiO/C full cell delivered a capacity of 400 mAh/g after 100 cycles at 0.5C. 
Density functional theory (DFT) calculation indicates that the cPAN is inert to the electrolyte, thus suppresses the 
catastrophic electrolyte decomposition caused by FeF2. DFT further suggests that the existence of vacancies on 
the carbon surface will cause H transfer reaction of the solvent, leading to the degradation of the electrolyte. This 
study provides a viable technology to enable high energy density FeF2 cathode-based LIBs for energy storage 
applications.   
1. Introduction 
Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are widely used in consumer electronics 
and electrical vehicles. However, for more demanding applications such 
as powering electric ships [1] and electric airplanes [2,3], much higher 
energy density is required. The main restriction to the energy density of 
LIBs is from the cathodes, whose specific capacity is limited by the 
intercalation electrochemistry. To this end, conversion-type cathodes 
such as iron-based fluorides (IBFs, typically FeF3 and FeF2) offer much 
higher specific capacity (572 mAh/g for FeF2 and 712 mAh/g for FeF3) 
than the current prevailing nickel-rich cathodes such as NMC (275 
mAh/g) used in electrical vehicle batteries [4–6]. Moreover, IBFs are 
cost effective, environmentally benign, and crustal abundance [7,8]. 
However, the development of IBFs is severely hindered by their 
intrinsic poor electron conductivity [9,10], the notorious sluggish ki­
netics in the conversion reaction [11], the serious active material 
dissolution [12], and continuous electrolyte consumption from irre­
versible cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI) formation and surface side 
reactions [13,14]. Among these deficiencies, electrolyte depletion 
caused by continuous electrolyte decomposition is an important reason 
for the performance degradation of IBFs. The continuous electrolyte 
consumption is catalyzed by nano Fe formed during lithiation [15,16]. 
Furthermore, the over-used conductive agent (mostly carbon materials) 
exacerbates electrolyte decomposition [17]. All the above mentioned 
factors inevitably deplete the electrolyte in the cell and result in pre­
mature failure of LIBs.

rials (up to 50%) or rational designing of IBFs/carbon composites [9,

19-21], conducting inert coatings [22,23], tuning the electrolyte 
composition by adjusting both the salts and solvents [14,24-26] are 
strategies to address the aforementioned issues in laboratory level half 
cells. However, how to mitigate excessive electrolyte consumption re­
mains an unresolved issue. The quantitative assessment of electrolyte 
loading on the performance of IBFs was largely overlooked. As IBFs 
decompose the electrolyte vigorously, a quantitive assessment of elec­
trolyte loading, especially in lean/finite electrolyte conditions should be 
essential to enhance the energy density at the cell/module level [8]. 
Moreover, it is worthy to know the influence of quantitative electrolyte 
loading on the full cell performance based on IBFs cathode and 
state-of-the-art silicon-based anode. 
In this work, the IBFs cycled in quantitative electrolyte loadings by 
rationally designing electrode structure and cathode/electrolyte inter­
face were systematically studied, and FeF2 was utilized as a represen­
tative IBF. FeF2 nanoparticles (>50 nm in diameter) were evenly 
embedded into cyclic polyacrylonitrile (hereafter denoted as cPAN) 
matrix, and the cPAN was utilized as a strong binder to compactly “glue” 
the active materials on the current collector with superior mechanical 
adhesion. To assess the electrolyte loading on the electrochemical per­
formance of the cells, three different electrolyte loadings, i.e. 15, 8, 5 μL 
mg−1 (ratios of electrolyte to FeF2), which are denoted as infinite, 
moderate and lean electrolytes hereafter, respectively, were tested. It 
showed that compared to bare FeF2, the FeF2-cPAN cathode exhibited a 
specific capacity over 500 mAh/g after 200 cycles under a high current 
density of 1C in infinite electrolyte, utilizing over 87% of the theoretical 
capacity of FeF2. When decreased the electrolyte to a lean condition, a 
specific capacity of 300 mAh/g after 150 cycles with an increased ICE of 
80.2% was attained. While the bare FeF2 degraded rapidly after only 30 
cycles in lean electrolyte condition with a rather low ICE (69.8%). Cryo- 
transmission electron microscopy (TEM) combined with density func­
tional theory (DFT) calculation revealed that cPAN effectively prevented 
the growth of excessive CEI on FeF2, and seemed to act as an inert 
cathode/electrolyte interface layer. Moreover, the decomposition of 
electrolyte catalyzed by super P carbon was also observed, which was 
largely ignored in the previous studies of IBFs. And a full cell with FeF2- 
cPAN as cathode and prelithiated SiO/C with stable solid electrolyte 
interphase (SEI) as anode was fabricated, which delivered a capacity of 
400 mAh/g after 100 cycles at 0.5C with moderate electrolyte. Our re­
sults demonstrate the crucial role of surface passivation of FeF2 in pre­
venting electrolyte decomposition in the IBFs cathode, which will 
accelerate the practical adoption of IBFs with higher specific energy for 
LIBs. 
2. Results and discussions 
2.1. Fabrication of the FeF2-cPAN composites cathodes 
In order to enhance the performance of FeF2 in finite electrolyte, 
passivating the interface between FeF2 and electrolyte is essential, as 
lithiated FeF2 catalyzes electrolyte decomposition, leading to rapid ca­
pacity decay [13,14]. In this context, we embedded nano-sized FeF2 into 
a cPAN matrix, and the details of FeF2-cPAN composites fabrication are 
shown in the experimental section. The cPAN was selected due to the 
retained polymeric properties and enhanced intrinsic electronic con­
ductivity after pyrolysis at 300 ◦C [27]. The structure evolution of cPAN 
under elevated temperature was confirmed by both X-ray photoelectron 
spectroscopy (XPS) and Raman measurements (Fig. S1), in which N1, 
N2, N3 and N4 represent the cyanic group, pyridinic group, substitu­
tional graphite group and graphite-like structure, respectively [27]. 
Based on the characteristics of cPAN, it may offer the following ad­
vantages: (1) Accommodation of volume changes by the elasticity of 
cPAN; (2) Facilitation of the electron transport by the enhanced elec­
tronic conductivity of cPAN. (3) Avoid direct contact between FeF2 and 
electrolyte by cPAN. These advantages were thoroughly confirmed, as 
will be elaborated later in this manuscript. 
The X-ray diffraction patterns (XRD) of PAN, cPAN and FeF2-cPAN 
composites are shown in Fig. 1a. All the Bragg peaks of FeF2-cPAN can 
be ascribed to the tetragonal FeF2 phase (JCPDS card no. 45–1062) 
without any significant impurities. The diffraction peak at 30◦of the 
pristine PAN disappeared after cyclization at 300 ◦C, suggesting the 
amorphization of cPAN. The amorphous cPAN may promote ion trans­
ports on the FeF2 surface [28]. A magnified view of the N 1 s XPS spectra 
on FeF2-cPAN exhibit two typical peaks at 397.2 (N2) and 398.5 eV 
(N3), confirming the cyclization of PAN (Fig. 1b). The powder color 
(FeF2-PAN) changed from yellowish brown to black (FeF2-cPAN) after 
cyclization, demonstrating further the existence of substitutional 
graphite group (N3) (Fig.S2). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was 
conducted to test the mechanical property of cPAN in the composites, 
and bare FeF2 was utilized as a comparison (Fig. 1c, d). For bare FeF2, an 
attractive force was generated when the cantilever tip approached the 
surface during loading. To this end the attractive force reached point A 
when the physical contact occurred. Upon further increasing the applied 
stress, the sample surface was supposed to deform until the contact force 
reached point D. During unloading, the contact stress released instantly 
and almost overlapped with the loading curve, showing an inconspic­
uous elastic behavior (Fig. 1c) [29]. For FeF2-cPAN, the attractive force 
reached point A1 when the tip reached cPAN surface (Fig. 1d). After the 
tip extruded into the cPAN, the reaction force suddenly decreased and 
showed a force plateau from point B to C, which could be attributed to 
the certain adsorption/dissipation energy effect by the soft and elastic 
cPAN layer [30,31], and the mechanical convolution between cPAN and 
FeF2 continued until reaching the maximum applied force (point D1). 
The reversing probe movement during unloading showed a hysteresis 
effect between the loading and unloading curves, demonstrating strong 
time-dependent viscoelastic behavior, similar to the case of polymeric 
materials’ indentation [32,33]. These AFM results indicate the elasticity 
of cPAN inheritted from PAN after cyclization. High-angle annular 
dark-field - scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) 
and cryo-TEM were conducted to reveal the microstructure of FeF2-c­
PAN composites. HAADF image of the FeF2 particles indicate an average 
size of 50 nm with a cross-linked bunch-like morphology (Fig. 1e). The 
corresponding elemental mapping shows that C and N element over­
lapped perfectly, forming a network with the FeF2 particles homoge­
nously dispersed in the cPAN network. Note that the widely used 
nano-sized FeF2 usually suffers from serious particle aggregation 
induced by the ultra-high surface energy, which generally reduces the 
capacity utilization [34,35]. The selected area electron diffraction 
(SAED) of the pristine FeF2-cPAN (inset in Fig. 1e) confirms the exis­
tence of a single phase with tetragonal FeF2 crystalline structure. 
Cryo-TEM images revealed that the overall FeF2 particle bunches were 
intimately confined within the amorphous cPAN shell with an average 
thickness of 5 nm (Fig. 1f and Fig. S3). The internal morphology of the 
FeF2-cPAN composites was further revealed in Fig. 1g and Fig. S4, 
showing the evenly distribution of FeF2 nanoparticles embedded in the 
cPAN network. This structure design may provide sufficient electron/ion 
pathways, prevent the electrolyte consumption, hinder the particle 
displacement or fusing during cycling, which may boost the electro­
chemical performance of the FeF2 cathode. 
2.2. Fabrication of a mechanical strong FeF2-cPAN cathode 
The structure integrity, mechanical strength, particle distribution 
and porosity of the electrode have significant impacts on the cycle sta­
bility, rate performance and lifespan of the LIBs [36]. Commercial 
electrodes typically utilize polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) as binder 
with excellent oxidation tolerance at relatively high potentials but prone 
to soften in electrolyte solutions [37]. Additionally, the non-conductive 
PVDF may not be ideal to stabilize an intrinsic insulating electrode with 
cathodes undergoing frequent large volume changes [38]. Thus, we 
excluded the use of PVDF in this work. Instead, the cPAN was developed 
as a multifunctional binder to stabilize the bulk electrode. Briefly, a

2.3. Electrochemical performance of FeF2-cPAN/Li half cells with 
quantitative electrolyte loadings 
The cycle performance of the FeF2-cPAN with quantitative electro­
lyte loadings in half cells was investigated. The anode was lithium metal. 
The electrolyte was lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) salt dis­
solved in dimethoxyethane (DME). Note that the LiFSI based electrolyte 
is prone to form a passivation layer on FeF2 [14,25]. We selected this 
electrolyte to minimize the electrolyte consumption by cPAN so as to 
underline the key role of surface engineering on cycling longevity of 
FeF2 in finite electrolyte.

Similar to the quantitative study of Li-S batteries [44], we started the 
test by defining an E/M ratio, where E represents the amount of elec­
trolyte while M represents metal fluoride, in this case FeF2. Three 
electrolytes with E/M = 15, 8, 5 μL mg−1, representing infinite, mod­
erate, lean electrolyte conditions, respectively, were tested. The batte­
ries for cycle stability tests were all electrochemically activated for 5 
cycles at 0.2C and then cycled at 1C. Bare FeF2 with PVDF binder was 
also tested for comparison. We firstly evaluated the cycle performance of 
FeF2-cPAN in infinite electrolyte (E/M = 15, Fig. 3a). It showed that the 
FeF2-cPAN cathode was stably cycled for over 200 cycles with a capacity 
retention over 500 mAh/g at 1C, realizing over 87% of the theoretical 
capacities of FeF2 at such a high current density, which is competitive to 
other state-of-the-art works in FeF2 [12,22,45]. The ultra-high capacity 
release might originate from the uniform dispersion of FeF2 in the cPAN 
matrix, sufficient permeation of electrolyte in the bulk electrode and 
enhanced physical contact between FeF2-cPAN and the current collec­
tor. In contrast, the capacity release of bare FeF2 (less than 300 mAh/g 
after 200 cycles) was significantly lower than that of FeF2-cPAN, and the 
former suffered from fast capacity decay during cycling. Contact loss 
during cycling in the PVDF based electrode might be the reason for the 
capacity fading (Fig. S6). These results are consistent with the adhesion 
test (Fig. 2f). 
Then, we tested the cells with moderate electrolyte loading (E/M = 8 
μL mg−1, Fig. 3b). It showed that the capacity of the cathode degraded 
gradually from over 600 mAh/g at the initial cycles to about 400 mAh/g 
after 150 cycles. The higher than theoretical capacity at the initial cycles 
was likely due to the small average FeF2 particle size (about 20–50 nm), 
which possess relatively large surface area that may trigger severe 
electrolyte decomposition at lower voltages. The electrolyte decompo­
sition may thus contribute to the total redox activity and increase the 
discharge capacity [14]. Moreover, the pure cPAN electrode without 
FeF2 can provide a capacity of 50 mAh/g at 0.2C (1C=572 mAh/g) 
(Fig. S7). The FeF2 with PVDF binder degraded slowly at the initial 40 
cycles and then suffered from a rather rapid fading after that with an 
irregular up and down bounce of the Coulombic efficiency, showing only 
100 mAh/g capacity retention after 150 cycles. The results could be 
attributed to the electrolyte depletion with cycling. To verify this, we 
further reduced the amount of electrolyte to a lean condition (E/M = 5 
μL mg−1, Fig. 3c). As expected, although the FeF2-cPAN cathode could 
still maintain 320 mAh/g after 150 cycles, the capacity of bare FeF2 
decreased rapidly within 30 cycles with little to none capacity retained. 
These data demonstrate the strong electrolyte quantity-dependent 
characters of FeF2. 
The ICE was also analyzed due to its critical role on batteries per­
formance [46]. Hitherto, the ICE of the IBFs was generally lower than 
80% which hinders its commercial adoption [14,20]. The low ICE was 
believed to be caused by the unfavorable CEI formation in the initial a 
few cycles. In the lean electrolyte condition, the ICE for FeF2-cPAN 
cathode was 80.2%, higher than that of bare FeF2 (69.8%) (Fig. 3d). 
Although the ICE for the FeF2-cPAN was increased, it was still far from 
reaching 100%. We speculated that a small amount of super P carbon 
may lead to the serious ether-based electrolyte decomposition and 
contribute to the low ICE. 
To confirm that cPAN coating helps improve the electrode kinetics, 
we conducted CV curves of FeF2 and FeF2-cPAN cathodes with different 
cycles (Fig. S8). It shows that the position of the oxidation/reduction 
peaks shift positively/negatively for the FeF2 cathode with cycling, 
which widen the voltage gap. For the FeF2-cPAN cathode, the shift of 
oxidation/reduction peaks is negligible. This result indicates that FeF2- 
cPAN cathode exhibits smaller polarization with cycling, which may be 
attributed to better reaction kinetics with the cPAN coating. Moreover, 
the voltage profiles of the 1st,2nd and 10th cycles of FeF2 and FeF2-cPAN 
cathode show that the latter has a smaller voltage hysteresis, further 
confirming the enhanced reaction kinetic with cPAN coating (Fig. S9).

To further confirm the enhanced conversion kinetics and electrolyte 
infiltration of the FeF2-cPAN cathode, the current density for long-term 
cycling was increased to 5C (Fig. 3e) in a moderate electrolyte condition. 
A capacity of 250 mAh/g after 300 cycles was maintained for the FeF2- 
cPAN, while bare FeF2 delivered only 110 mAh/g capacity after 130 
cycles. The rate performances showed that the FeF2-cPAN cathode 
delivered the discharge capacities of 675, 610, 525, 460, 375, 300 and 
180 mAh/g at the rates of 0.2, 1, 5, 10, 20, 40 and 80C, respectively. The 
capacity could recover to 580 mAh/g after the rate was reduced back to 
0.2C (Fig. 3f). In comparison, the bare FeF2 showed lower specific ca­
pacities at all the tested rates. Generally, the capacities retention de­
creases with increasing current densities. However, the discharge 
capacity of FeF2-cPAN cathode was higher at 5C than that at 1C after 
150 cycles (Fig. 3c, e), indicating that the discharge capacity could be 
increased by sufficient electrolyte in the electrode even at high C rates. 
The enhanced capacity might originate from capacitive activity. To this 
end, the cyclic voltammetry (CV) studies of FeF2-cPAN were performed 
at different sweep rates to discriminate the capacity contribution from 
surface capacitive and diffusion-controlled processes [47] (Fig. S10). 
The result showed that about 83.4% of capacity could be attributed to 
capacitive contribution for the FeF2-cPAN electrode at high scanning 
rate of 1.8 mV/s (Fig. S10c). The corresponding capacitive capacity 
between the FeF2-cPAN and bare FeF2 is summarized in Fig. S10d. These 
data indicates that the majority of the charge storage in the FeF2-cPAN 
electrode was pseudocapacitive at high rates. The amorphous cPAN with 
N3 component may be beneficial to charge storage [48,49]. 
Note that the performance under lean electrolyte condition is much 
lower than the excessive electrolyte even for the FeF2-cPAN cathode. We 
speculated that the electrolyte was consumed during the cycling, and 
there were many components in the electrode that may cause the con­
sumption of the electrolyte, such as the conductive agent (super P). To 
further validate this point, we tested the cycle performance of FeF2- 
cPAN electrode with different super P contents under moderate elec­
trolyte (8 uL mg−1) (Fig. S11). The results show that the initial discharge 
capacity of the cathode was relatively low when the content of super P 
was only 5 wt% in the cathode. When increasing the super P content 
from 10% to 30wt%, the initial discharge capacities of all the cells in­
crease to the same level due to the enhanced electronic conductivity.

2.4. Tracking the electrolyte consumption 
To clarify the stabilization mechanism, we conducted cryo-TEM 
studies on the samples after different cycles. Cryo-TEM was operated 
at near liquid nitrogen temperature which minimizes radiation damage 
to the surface layer by high energy electron beams [52,53]. The pristine 
FeF2 showed a pure crystalline structure with only a few atomic layers 
thick amorphous phase (Fig. 4a and Fig. S13). After the 1st discharge, a 3 
nm thickness CEI layer with poor crystallinity formed on the surface of 
FeF2 (Fig. 4b). The surface crystal structure was identified to be Fe3O4 
(Fig. S14), similar to our previously reported results [54]. However, an 
amorphous CEI layer with about 40 nm in thickness after 20 cycles for 
bare FeF2 was observed (Fig. 4c and Fig. S15). Thick CEI formation on 
the bare FeF2 was generally observed in DME based electrolyte [14]. 
These amorphous layers were formed by electrolyte decomposition 
which continuously consumed the electrolyte [12,14]. CEI formation 
was further proved by the elemental mapping of merged F/S, F/O and 
F/Fe images, which showed the S and O-rich species on FeF2 particles 
(Fig. S16). Surprisingly, a similar thick amorphous layer was also formed 
on the surface of super P carbon after cycling (Fig. 4d,e). The vigorous 
amorphous layer formation on carbon was largely ignored in the pre­
vious studies of IBFs with high amount of carbon. In comparison, the 
well-designed FeF2-cPAN cathode showed no apparent CEI formation on 
the cPAN shell (Fig. 4f and Figs. S17,18). The local HAADF image from 
Fig. 4g and corresponding elemental mapping showed weak S signal 
around FeF2 particles (Fig. S19), indicating negligible electrolyte con­
sumption on the CEI formation. Our previous study showed that a Fe3O4 
layer was inevitably formed on the cycled FeF2 in ether electrolyte [54]. 
In this study, the HAADF images revealed that a thin Fe3O4 layer was 
observed on both the bare FeF2 (Fig. S14) and the FeF2-cPAN cathode 
(Fig. 4g, h) in finite electrolyte, indicating the Fe3O4 shell formation may 
be independent on the surface conditions. The oxidation of extruded 
highly reactive Fe atoms by electrolyte may be responsible for the Fe3O4 
formation [11,25,55]. 
We further performed XPS analysis on the cycled surface of bare FeF2 
and FeF2-cPAN cathodes after 20 cycles (Figs. 4i-p). For the bare FeF2 
cathode, the intense sp3 signal (284.6 eV) in the C 1 s spectra indicates

2.5. Density functional theory (DFT) calculation on H-transfer of DME 
molecule 
We used DFT to investigate the degradation pathways for DME 
molecule when exposed to the fabricated electrodes. Confronting with 
active electrode materials, most of electrolytes exhibit lower oxidation 
potentials than their intrinsic values [58], since newly emerging H 
transfer to catalytic cathodes surface reaction (H-transfer reaction) will 
determine the upper limit of electrochemical window [59]. For DME 
molecule, there are three kinds of binding sites that can potentially 
donate protons. The first is from the -CH3 group decomposition, which 
will form -*CH2 and *H (* represents the adsorption site). The second is 
from -CH2-, which also needs to break the C–H bond to form -*CH- and 
*H, while -O- can be directly bound without decomposition reaction. 
Therefore, the decomposition reaction was likely predominated by the 
H-transfer from the -CH3 and -CH2- group. The DME decomposition on 
the surface of Fe3O4, super P carbon with different vacancy state and 
cPAN was calculated. 
The calculation of Fe3O4 was considered due to the Fe3O4 shell for­
mation on cycled FeF2 surface (Fig. 4e,f). The results show that Fe3O4 
preferentially combines with O with a binding energy of −1.12 eV 
(Fig. 5a), indicating that the presence of Fe3O4 will not cause the H- 
transfer reaction. But a stronger O combination may lead to C–O frac­
ture and cause the decomposition of DME solvent [60]. 
Cryo-TEM investigation indicated that super P carbon decomposes 
electrolyte easily (Fig. 4e), so we also considered the effect of super P on 
the DME decomposition. The results showed that super P with defect 
free surface inclines to combine with O with the absence the H-transfer 
reaction (Fig. S20a). While the existed vacancies on the super P surface 
may preferentially combine with -CH3 in the DME molecule with a 
binding energy of −0.64 eV for single vacancy (Fig. S20b) and −1.16 eV 
for double vacancies (Fig. 5b), which will trigger the H-transfer reaction 
and cause DME decomposition. Note that the perfect defect free carbon 
surface almost does not existent [61]. 
The H transfer reaction between cPAN containing graphite nitrogen 
and pyridinic nitrogen and DME was also calculated. Interestingly, the 
pyridinic nitrogen and graphite nitrogen structure preferentially bind 
only with O in DME solvent (Fig. 5c,d). Moreover, the binding energies 
of pyridine nitrogen and graphite nitrogen with O are −0.38 eV and 
−0.45 eV, respectively, which are lower than the value of Fe3O4 with O 
(−1.12 eV), avoiding a stronger O combination may lead to C–O frac­
ture in DME. The binding energy of DME molecules with -CH2- and -CH3 
was positive, suggesting the reaction is unfavorable, or in other words, 
there is little tendency to trigger side reactions. This result is consistent 
with the results from Fig. 4f, which shows little CEI formation on the 
cPAN surface.

important step towards their commercial adoption [62,63]. Hitherto, 
the understanding of electrochemistry of IBFs in full cells is scarce.

and the cell reached only about 30% of the theoretical capacity of FeF3

wide adoption in practical application [68]. 
Fig. 6b shows the discharge capacity of the assembled FeF2-cPAN| 
LiFSI/DME|SiO/C full cell, and the SiO/C was pre-lithiated in ether- 
based electrolyte (LiFSI/DME). The full cell delivered an initial 
discharge capacity of 565 mAh/g and faded to 300 mAh/g in the first 50 
cycles at 0.5C. The rapid fading phenomenon may possibly be resulted 
from the limited lithium utilization in SiO/C anode as the FeF2-cPAN 
exhibited excellent cycle stability in the Li||FeF2-cPAN half-cell with 
infinite lithium sources (Fig. 3a). Thus, we intentionally replaced the 
cycled SiO/C anode with a fresh SiO/C anode. Surprisingly, the initial 
capacity recovered to 515 mAh/g. The following capacity degradation 
was identical to the first SiO/C electrode, showing rapid capacity decay 
in the initial 50 cycles. The voltage plateaus of the 2nd, 50th and 51th 
cycles were plotted. The 51th cycle also repeated the 1st cycle after the 
replacement of the cycled SiO/C with a fresh SiO/C (Fig. 6c-e). The 
results showed that the voltage plateaus dropped from 2.1 V at the 2nd 
cycle to 1.75 V at the 50th cycle, which returned to 2.1 V in the 51th cycle 
(Fig. 6c-e). These results indicated that the polarization of the full cell 
was caused by the problem in the anode, which also lead to the capacity 
decay. The EIS results show that the charge transfer resistance increased 
after 50th cycle, and decreased after replacing the anode (51th cycle) 
(Fig. S21), indicating that the decaying anode structure will lead to the 
increase of impedance, resulting in polarization of the full cell. 
Considering the significant impact of solid electrolyte interface (SEI) 
on the polarization and cycle stability of the SiO/C anode [69], we 
prelithiated the SiO/C in different electrolytes. Generally, a favorable 
SEI may be formed in carbonate electrolyte for SiO/C [70] while the 
LiFSI/DME electrolyte was used in our prelithiation. SEM images and 
the corresponding elemental mapping showed that the S rich 
by-products formed due to the electrolyte decomposition on the surface 
of SiO/C anode after 50 cycles (Fig. S22). The results suggest that the 
excessive decomposition of electrolyte or continuous regeneration of SEI 
lead to continuous consumption of lithium ion at the anode. Thus, it is 
reasonable to speculate that the ether-based electrolyte might not be a 
good choice to prelithiate the SiO/C anode. We also evaluated the cycle 
performance of the SiO/C in the carbonate and ether electrolyte. The 
cycle stability of SiO/C was indeed improved in the carbonate electro­
lyte (Fig. S23). The charge and discharge curves revealed a large over­
potential at the initial lithiation in ether electrolyte, implying the 
sluggish reaction kinetics (Fig. S24). These results suggested the SiO/C 
was incapable to form stable SEI in pre-lithiation with ether electrolyte. 
A well prelithiated SiO/C with a favorable SEI is the key to stabilize the 
FeF2-cPAN|LiFSI/DME|SiO/C full cell.

cycled the full cell. As expected, the full cell achieved a high discharge 
capacity of 400 mAh/g, a high capacity retention of 87.6% and long

3. Conclusion 
We report a strategy to enable high performance FeF2 cathode in lean 
electrolyte by embedding the FeF2 nanoparticles into a cPAN matrix. 
The cPAN engineered FeF2 cathode surface has the following merits. 
1 The cPAN forms a network with good electronic and ionic conduc­
tivity, coating on the surface of FeF2 with a thickness of 5 nm, 
providing viable electron/ion transport for FeF2.  
2 Cryo-TEM combining with DFT calculation proves that cPAN is inert 
to the ether-based electrolyte, thus preventing the catastrophic 
electrolyte decomposition.

4. Experimental section 
4.1. Synthesis of FeF2 nanoparticles 
In a typical synthesis process [14,54], excess amount of Fe powder 
(2.0 g, Sigma-Aldrich, 99%) was reacted with 10 mL H2SiF6 aqueous 
solution (25wt%, Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h. The mixture was then 
centrifuged to eliminate the residual solids. The as-received FeSiF6 
aqueous solution was diluted to 20 mL with distilled water. Then, the 
FeSiF6 aqueous solution was dried and annealed at 260 ◦C for 4 h under 
argon flow to obtain pristine FeF2. 
4.2. Fabrication of electrodes 
FeF2-cPAN cathode and electrode: the obtained pristine FeF2 parti­
cles were mixed with PAN (Mw = 150,000 g/mol, Sigma-Aldrich) and 
super P conductive agent with an 80:10:10 mass ratio using a mortar and 
pestle. The solid mixture was then stirred in N, N-dimethylformamide 
(DMF) (Aladdin, 99.8%, anhydrous) via magnetic stirring for about 12 h 
to produce a viscous slurry. The slurry was then casted onto an 
aluminum foil current collector and dried at 80 ◦C for 4 h. The dried 
electrode was then heated at 300 ◦C under N2 flow for 10 h to obtain 
FeF2-cPAN composites on the current collector. Electrodes with bare 
FeF2 as cathode was fabricated by conventional method for comparison. 
Briefly, slurry was made by mixing FeF2, PVDF binder and Super P 
carbon with a weight ratio of 60:10:30 in N-Methyl pyrrolidone (NMP) 
solvent (Sigma-Aldrich, anhydrous, 99.5%). The resulted slurry was 
casted on aluminum foil via doctor blade and dried at 110 ◦C in vacuum. 
The SiO/C-based anodes: Typically, SiO/C powder (Canard) with 
theoretical capacity of 450 mAh/g, acetylene black and PVDF were 
blended in NMP solvent to form a slurry with a weight ratio of 85:5:10, 
respectively. The prepared slurry was then casted onto a copper foil and 
vacuum dried at 100 ◦C for 12 h. 
All the working electrodes were punched into circular shape with a 
diameter of 11 mm. 
4.3. Electrochemical measurements of half cells and full cells 
Half cells testing: the average mass loadings of FeF2 in FeF2-cPAN 
electrodes was about 3 mg/cm2. Batteries were assembled with 2032 
type coin cell with lithium metal as anode. Celgard 2400 (Celgard, USA) 
was used as separator. 3 M LiFSI in DME was used as electrolyte. The 
electrochemical performance was tested using battery testing system 
(LAND, CT3001A) over the voltage window of 1–4 V (vs Li+/Li). CV tests 
were conducted on a Princeton 4000 electrochemical workstation at 
different scanning rate from 0.2 to 1.8 mV/s in the voltage range of 1–4 
V (vs Li+/Li). 
SiO/C treatment and full cells testing: the prelithiation and SEI for­
mation was conducted by cycling the SiO/C once at a current density of 
0.1C in a voltage range of 0.01–3.0 V (vs Li+/Li), followed by dis­
charging to 0.01 V with Li metal as anode. The N/P ratio was controlled 
at 1.5:1. Celgard 2500 was used as separator. The carbonate electrolyte 
was prepared by dissolving 1.0 M LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate (EC) 
(Aladdin, 99.8%, anhydrous) and diethyl carbonate (DMC) (Aladdin, 
99.9%, anhydrous) (1:1 by volume) with 5% fluoroethylene carbonate 
(FEC) (Aladdin, 99.9%, anhydrous) additive. The prelithiated SiO/C 
anodes with constructed SEI were then retrieved by disassembling the 
treated cells and washing with dimethyl carbonate (DMC) (Aladdin, 
99.9%, anhydrous). The retrieved SiO/C anodes were paired with FeF2- 
cPAN cathodes in 2032 type coin cells with the aforementioned sepa­
rator and ether electrolyte (3 M LiFSI in DME) to study the electro­
chemical performance. The full cells were cycled at various current rates 
in a voltage range of 1–4 V. All cells were tested at 25 ◦C. 
4.4. Materials characterization 
The XRD testing was performed in a Bruker D8 advance diffrac­
tometer in a transmission mode with Cu Ka X-ray source operated at 
room temperature. Raman spectra were obtained with a 532 nm 
wavelength laser. XPS (ESCALAB 250Xi) was carried out to investigate 
the chemical state of the elements on the sample surface. The surface 
morphology and internal structure morphology was observed in a FIB 
system (Helios G4 CX, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The microstructures 
were observed with a transmission electron microscope (TEM, Titan G2, 
300 kV, Thermo Fisher Scientifc). The HAADF-STEM images and 
elemental mapping were acquired in a Cs-corrected scanning trans­
mission electron microscope (Titan cubed Themis Z 300 kV, Thermo 
Fisher scientific), which was equipped with a DCOR condenser lens 
spherical aberration corrector for forming a fine electron probe. For 
Cryo-TEM sample preparation, the cycled electrodes were washed with 
DME followed by ultra-sonication in DMC solvent for 30 min. The 
samples dispersed in DMC were drop casted onto a TEM micro-grid in 
the glove box and frozen transferred to a Fischione MODEL 2550 cryo 
holder for observation without any exposure to air. All Cryo-TEM images 
were acquired at low temperature (90 K) under low dose. 
4.5. Theoretical calculation 
All DFT calculations were implemented in the Vienma ab initio 
simulation package (VASP) [71,72]. The electron exchange and corre­
lation energy were treated within the generalized gradient approxima­
tion in the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional (GGA-PBE) [73]. The 
calculations were performed with a plane-wave basis set defined by a 
kinetic energy cutoff of 450 eV. The k-point sampling was obtained from 
the Monkhorst-Pack scheme with a (3 × 3 × 1) mesh for optimization 
and the calculations of electronic structure. The geometry optimization 
and energy calculation are finished when the electronic self-consistent 
iteration and force reached 10−5 eV and 0.02 eV Å−1, respectively.
