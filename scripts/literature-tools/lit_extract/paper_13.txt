ABSTRACT
Sodium (Na) metal batteries (SMBs) using Na anode are potential “beyond lithium” electrochemical technology for future energy
storage  applications.  However,  uncontrollable  Na  dendrite  growth  has  plagued  the  application  of  SMBs.  Understanding  Na
deposition mechanisms, particularly the early stage of Na deposition kinetics, is critical to enable the SMBs. In this context, we
conducted in situ observations of the early stage of electrochemical Na deposition. We revealed an important electrochemical
Ostwald  ripening  (EOR)  phenomenon  which  dictated  the  early  stage  of  Na  deposition.  Namely,  small  Na  nanocrystals  were
nucleated  randomly,  which  then  grew.  During  growth,  smaller  Na  nanocrystals  were  contained  by  bigger  ones  via  EOR.  We
observed  two  types  of  EOR  with  one  involving  only  electrochemical  reaction  driven  by  electrochemical  potential  difference
between  bigger  and  smaller  nanocrystals;  while  the  other  being  dominated  by  mass  transport  governed  by  surface  energy
minimization. The results provide new understanding to the Na deposition mechanism, which may be useful for the development
of SMB for energy storage applications.
KEYWORDS
in situ, Na deposition, electrochemical Ostwald ripening (EOR), Na nanocrystals
 
 
1    Introduction
Ostwald ripening is a crystal growth mechanism in which larger
nanocrystals grow at the expense of smaller ones, which are
progressively dissolved [1–9]. Electrochemical Ostwald ripening
(EOR) involves electron and ion transport from a smaller
nanocrystal to a larger one. Henglein and Plieth first explored the
physicochemical and electrochemical properties of metal
nanoparticles, respectively [10–12]. Plieth predicted a negative
shift in the Ag standard electrode potential and a decrement in the
work function of small (< 25 nm) metallic particles [11]. The
standard electrode potential Ep of a small nanocrystal shifts
negatively via the following equation [1, 2, 11]
Ep = Ebulk −2γVm/zFr
(1)
where γ is the surface tension; Vm is the molar volume; z is the ion
exchange numbers; F is Faraday’s constant; r is the radius.
As the electrochemical potential of a smaller nanocrystal is
more negative than a larger one, the smaller nanocrystal is easier
to be oxidized than the larger one. For EOR to occur, there must
be a fast electron transport path between the particles via either
direct contact or an indirect current collector, and a fast metal ion
transport channel between the metal particles through an
electrolyte. The occurrence of EOR will cause uneven metal
deposition, which critically affects the performance of sodium
(Na) metal batteries (SMBs) or other metal ion batteries (MIBs)
[13, 14]. Herein, we in situ image an EOR phenomenon which
dictates the early stage of Na and lithium (Li) deposition. Namely,
small Na or Li nanocrystals are nucleated randomly, which then
grow. During growth, smaller Na or Li nanocrystals are contained
by bigger ones via EOR.

2.1.2    Synthesis of hollow CNTs
Hollow CNTs were fabricated through a typical chemical vapor
deposition method. Anodic aluminum oxide (AAO) membranes
with a pore size about 200 nm were first placed in a tube furnace.
After that, they were heated in an argon (Ar) atmosphere up to
700 °C to deposit a carbon layer (~ 24 nm) via C3H6
decomposition at an atmospheric pressure. The carbon-coated
AAO membranes were etched away with 10% hydrofluoric acid
(HF) and then washed with deionized water several times. The
final products were dried at 60 °C in vacuum. A bulk dry hollow
CNT was ultrasonically dispersed in ethanol and then dropped
onto a clean glass slide with a half-copper mesh and dried in a
furnace [18, 19].
 
2.2    In situ electrochemical deposition
For in situ observations of EOR during Na and Li deposition, an
electrochemical deposition platform was constructed in an
environmental transmission electron microscope (ETEM) based
on an all solid state cell setup [16, 20, 21]. The setup consists of an
arc discharged MWCNT or hollow CNT attached to an
aluminum (Al) rod as the cathode, and Na or Li metal scratched
on a sharp Al tip as the counter electrode. Naturally formed
Na2CO3 or Li2CO3 layer on the surface of Na or Li metal acted as
the solid electrolyte, respectively (Fig. S1(a) in the Electronic
Supplementary Material (ESM)). The electrochemical setup was
mounted into a TEM–scanning tunneling microscopy (STM)
holder (Pico Femto FE-F20) inside a glove box. Then the holder
was sealed in a home-built air-tight bag filled with dry argon and
transferred to the ETEM. The total time of air exposure was less
than 2 s, which limited the thickness of the oxidization layer on
the surface of the metal Na or Li. Upon applying a negative bias to
the MWCNT against the Na or Li electrode, electrochemical
deposition was initiated. During the experiments, CO2 gas (purity
higher than 99.99%) with a pressure of 1 mbar was flowed into the
ETEM chamber, which promoted the formation of a thin Na2CO3
solid electrolyte interface (SEI) layer on the surface of the
deposited sodium metal, mimicing the SEI layer formation in the
anode of liquid electrolyte based batteries. The formation of
Na2CO3 SEI layer stablized the deposited Na under electron beam
illumination, thus facilitating the TEM observations.

Overall, we observed two types of EOR, as shown schematically
in Fig. 4, which were hereafter referred to as EOR1 and EOR2. In
EOR1, the nanocrystals were far away from each other, and
during the growth process, the smaller ones shrank continuously
until they totally disappeared. While the bigger one grew rapidly at
the consumption of the smaller ones (Figs. 4(a) and 4(b)). In
EOR2, two or three neighboring nanocrystals emerged almost at
the same time, yet they appeared to be separated by a thin surface
layer (Fig. 4(c)). Upon further growth, the thin shell was broken
and the two or three nanocrystals joined together to become one
larger crystal (Figs. 4(d) and 4(e)). We believe that EOR1 is a pure
electrochemical process, as the following electrochemical reaction
must have occurred: Na → Na+ + e− in the smaller nanocrystals and
the reverse reaction Na+ + e− → Na in the bigger nanocrystals.

MWCNT is indeed a mixed ionic and electronic conductor
(MIEC) [22]. We did a similar experiment with an Ag nanowire
instead of a MWCNT as the working electrode. Different from
MWCNT, the Ag nanowire can only conduct electrons, so we
only observed the deposition and EOR of Na at the Ag/Na2CO3
contact point (Fig. S8 in the ESM) but not in the middle of the Ag
nanowire. In contrast, because of the MIEC nature of the
MWCNT, the Na dendrites can nucleate and grow at both the
MWCNT/Na2CO3 contact point and the middle of the MWCNT
(Fig. 1). This once again proves that the EOR1 process needs to
have both ion and electron transport pathways. In EOR2, we
believe that the initial stage involved electrochemical reactions
similar to that in EOR1, however, once the shell separating the two
nanocrystals was broken, the merging between the two
nanocrystals was similar to the chemical Ostwald ripening,
meaning that no electrochemical reaction may have taken place,
and it was mostly a mass transfer from the smaller one to the
bigger one due to surface energy minimization.
Figure 4(f) shows a plot of the shift in the standard electrode
potential of small Na nanocrystals as a function of radius
according to Eq. (1). The surface energy γ = 0.21 J/m2 is used for
Na [23]. Obviously, the deflection of the standard electrode
potential is inversely related to the particle size. Once the radius of
the nanocrystal reaches 103 nm, the negative shift of standard
electrode potential will be less than 1 mV. With continued Na
deposition, even though the particle radius increases several times,
the standard electrode potential only changes slightly. Apparently,
the driving force for the EOR is the electrochemical potential
difference between the smaller nanocrystals and the bigger ones
according to Eq. (1). Following Eq. (1), the potential difference
between the nanocrystal Na and bulk Na can be appreciable,
namely, for a nanocrystal with a size of 2 nm, its electrochemical
potential is about 51 mV lower than that of bulk Na (Fig. 4(f)).
Similarly, the electrochemical potential of a Li nanocrystal with a
size of 2 nm is about 57 mV lower than that of bulk Li (The
surface energy γ = 0.43 J/m2 is used for Li) [1, 2, 24]. It should be
noted that once the nanocrystal grows to a larger size, nucleation
of smaller Na nanocrystals is rare if not obsolete, suggesting that
EOR is a phenomenon taking place only at the early stage of Na
deposition. EOR may lead to anomalous grain growth and cause
non-uniform Na deposition, which is detrimental for SMBs. Thus
uniform surface structure or uniform electric field distribution
must be implemented in the Na anode to avoid anomalous grain
growth and non-uniform Na deposition.
 
4    Conclusions
In conclusion, we observed two types of EOR (EOR1 and EOR2)
during the electrochemical deposition of Na and Li. In EOR1,
large and small Na nanocrystals nucleated far away from each
other, and later on the larger nanocrystal grew at the expense of
the small nanocrystal via electrochemical Ostwald ripening. In
EOR2, two or three Na and Li nanocrystals contacting one
another yet were separated by a thin surface Na2CO3 or Li2CO3
layer, respectively, and the large one grew at the expense of the
small one via electrochemical Ostwald ripening similar to EOR1
initially. Upon further growth of the large one, it broke the shell
near the contact of the nanocrystals, and the growth then
appeared to be chemical Ostwald ripening (i.e., mass transfer
occurred directly from the small one to the large one due to
surface energy minimization). EOR occurred only at the early
stage of Na and Li deposition, which may lead to anomalous grain
growth and was detrimental for Na and Li metal batteries.
