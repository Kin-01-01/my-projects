ABSTRACT
Lithium-oxygen (Li-O2) batteries have been considered as an ideal solution to solving the global energy crisis. Silver (Ag) or Ag-
based  catalyst  have  been  extensively  studied  due  to  their  high  catalytic  activities  in  Li-O2  batteries.  However,  it  remains  a
challenge  to  track  the  catalytic  mechanism  during  the  charge/discharge  process.  Here,  a  nanoscale  processing  method  was
used  to  assemble  a  Li-O2  nanobattery  in  an  aberration-corrected  environmental  transmission  electron  microscope  (ETEM),
where a single Ag nanowire (NW) was used as catalyst for O2 electrode. A visualization of the lithium ion insertion process during
the  electrochemical  reactions  were  achieved  in  this  nanobattery.  Numerous  Ag  nanoparticles  (NPs)  were  observed  on  the
surface  of  the  Ag  NW,  which  were  covered  by  the  discharge  product  Li2O2.  By  simultaneously  studying  the  evolution  of  the
interface and the phase transformation, it can be concluded that those Ag NPs wrapped around Ag NW acted as catalyst during
the subsequent charge/discharge reaction. Based on those studies, Ag NPs decorated on porous carbon were synthesized, and
it can simultaneously improve the cycling stability (100 cycles) and the maximum specific capacity (17,371 mAh·g−1 at a current
density of 100 mA·g−1) in a coin cell Li-O2 battery. This study suggests that nanoscale Ag may be a promising catalyst for Li-O2
battery.

1    Introduction
Li-O2 batteries have attracted extensive attention since E. L.
Littauer proposed the concept [1]. The battery is a representative
of new energy storage equipment owing to its ultra-high
theoretical energy density (3,505 Wh·kg−1) [2–5]. However, the
development of Li-O2 battery is still in a relatively preliminary
state due to its poor cycle stability, large overpotential, sluggish
kinetics and many parasitic reactions [6–10]. The effective ways to
alleviate these problems are developing catalytic materials
including carbon materials, transition metal oxides and noble
metals [11–13]. Although carbon materials have high electrical
conductivity and excellent performance of oxygen reductive
reaction (ORR), they also derive a serios parasitic reactions so that
cause poor capacity of oxygen evolution reaction (OER) [14–16].
Transition metal oxides catalysts exhibit high stability and activity
for electrode, but electrical conductivity is unsatisfactory, which
limits the cycling performance and rate capacity [17, 18]. Here,
noble metals show both wonderful performance of ORR and
OER, the comprehensive catalytic performance is higher that
could reduce voltage polarizations and improve reaction kinetics
[19].
As one of the noble metals, Ag demonstrates an outstanding
catalytic performance but low price [20]. In order to enhance the
active sites, researchers have designed plenty of Ag materials,
including Ag nanosheets, Ag nanowires (NWs) and Ag
nanoparticles (NPs) [21–23]. Usually, adding catalytic sites can
promote a significant effect on the performance. Nanoscale
particles are appropriate choice. Nonetheless, the nanoscale Ag
particles are easily agglomerated, which need suitable basis
materials to alleviate this problem. Lu et al. [24] decorated Ag NPs
uniformly in porous carbon that enhanced Ag catalytic
performance. However, although preventing the agglomeration of
Ag NPs, how to choose a suitable particle size is also quite
important. Lu et al. [25] reported that Ag nanoparticles loaded on
carbon-free cathode materials and displayed sufficient catalytic
performance. Moreover, monatomic atoms have always been a
research hotspot. Zhou et al. [26] reported that Ag single atoms
had abundant catalytic sites and a prominent catalytic capacity.
But the synthetic process was complicated. Hence, size effect is
also a considered factor for catalytic capacity. For instance, it has
been reported that Au particles had the optimum catalytic
performance at the size of 3 nm for selective hydrogenation of
acetylene in excess ethylene [27]. Therefore, the optimal catalytic
capacity could be locked by adjusting the particle size of catalysts.
SBA-15 mesoporous materials existed a good limited effect [28]. It
would lock Ag NPs at a suitable range that released better catalytic
ability. It is crucial to achieve high-performance catalysis by tuning

2    Experimental
 
2.1    Synthesis of Ag NWs
Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, 0.6 g) was dispersed in 25 mL of
ethylene glycol under vigorous stirring for 1 h. Then, 0.8 g of
AgNO3 was added to the solution slowly and stirred for another
3 h. Subsequently, the solution was transferred to a 100 mL Teflon-
lined autoclave and maintained at 160 °C for 3 h. After cooling
down to ambient temperature, the resulting product was
thoroughly rinsed, and then dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for
12 h to obtain Ag NWs.
 
2.2    Nano-scale battery
The charge/discharge reactions of Ag NWs were investigated in a
Li-O2 nanobattery by using an ETEM (FEI-Titan G2 ETEM,
300 kV), where an Ag NW, a bulk lithium, and a natural Li2O
layer were used as catalyst for Li-O2 electrode, counter electrode,
and solid electrolyte, respectively. (1) A few drops of ultrasonic
dispersed Ag NWs were dripped on the aluminum tip as the
working electrode. (2) In a glove box filled with Ar atmosphere,
metal Li scratched on a tungsten (W) tip was used as counter
electrode and Li2O naturally formed on metal Li acted as solid
electrolyte. (3) Sealed nanobattery in transmission electron
microscopy-scanning tunneling microscopy (TEM-STM) sample
rod (Pico Femto FE-F2) in Ar environment and quickly
transferred into ETEM in Ar dry airtight bags that limited Li2O
forming on the surface of Li metal. During the process of
charge/discharge, at a pressure of 1 mbar (purity is higher 99.99%)
O2 gas flowed into specimen chamber. The Ag NW was
manipulated to approach the Li2O layer, and then a potential was
applied to the nanowire versus the Li metal electrode to either
charge or discharge.
 
2.3    Synthesis of Ag NPs
Ag NPs were fabricated by using a template method [33]. In this
experiment, SBA-15 (2 g) and anhydrous ethanol (100 mL) were
first dissolved to form a solution (A). At the same time, glycine
(2 g) and deionized water (100 mL) were mixed to form a solution
(B). Then AgNO3 (0.85 mmol) was dissolved in 10 mL deionized
water and it was  sonicated for about 10 min. After that, citric acid
(5 mmol) was slowly added to the AgNO3 solution under
ultrasonic status to form a solution (C). Solution (C) was added
dropwise into solution (B) with vigorous stirring at room
temperature. After the mixed solution was stirred constantly for
1 h, transferred it into solution (A) under the vigorous stirring for
another 1 h. The obtained solution was transferred to a round-
bottom flask in oil bath and maintaining at 80 °C for 3 h.
Afterwards, solvent was evaporated with a rotary evaporator to
obtain precursor. After cooling down to the ambient temperature,
precursor was collected and dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for
12 h. Subsequently, the precursor was annealed at 650 °C for 2 h
with nitrogen flow of 5 mL·min−1 in a pipe-type oven. Finally, the
black solid power was soaked in 6 mol NaOH solution at 60 °C for
24 h to remove SBA-15 to obtain Ag/C nanocomposites.

2.5    Materials characterization
The crystal structures of Ag/C nanocomposites were characterized
by a Rigaku D/max 2500pc X-ray diffractometer (XRD), utilizing
a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 0.15406 nm) operated at 40 kV and
30 mA with a step scan of 0.02° (20°–90°, 4°·min−1). Morphology
of the constructed Ag/C nanocomposites were characterized by
ETEM, TEM, energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy and
high-angle annular dark field (HAADF). The porous properties
such as the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area and pore
size distribution was analyzed from the nitrogen adsorption and
desorption isotherms obtained using a surface area and
porosimetry analyzer (V-Sorb 2800P, Gold APP Instruments
Corporation China). Raman spectra were performed on a
Renishaw Gloucestershire at a laser wavelength of 514 nm.

3.1    Morphology  evolution  of  the  Ag  NW  in  a

To illuminate the catalytic mechanism of Ag in Li-O2 battery, a
nanoscale characterization technique was used to construct a Li-

has two probes (Al tip and W tip), as shown in Fig. 1(a). The Ag
nanowire glued to the Al tip was used as the catalyst for O2

surface of the Li was used as the solid electrolyte. Those electrodes
were assembled into a holder (Pico Femto FE-F2) of TEM-STM
inside a glovebox, and then transferred to an ETEM (FEI, Titan
G2, 300 kV), and a Li-O2 nanobattery was assembled in ETEM,
where an Ag nanowire can be used as the working electrode,
which was manipulated to contact with the Li2O solid electrolyte.
When an appropriate operating potential is applied to this Li-O2
nanobattery, the lithiation process can be visualized in real-time.

transformation of the electrode in the Li-O2 battery. The influence
of Ag catalyst on the Li-O2 battery performance will be
understood and used as scientific basis and design strategies to
improve the electrochemical properties Li-O2 battery.
 
4    Conclusion
Li-O2 nanobatteries were assembled in an ETEM to explore the
reaction mechanism of Ag catalysts. Our results indicate that the
Ag NW had a core/shell structure with the discharge products
coating layer wrapped around the inner Ag NW after the first
discharge. Concurrent with the discharge and charge process,
thickness of the coating layer wrapped around Ag NW expand
and shrank, respectively. Numerous single crystal Ag NPs are
emerged in the coating layer around closely to the Ag NW,
indicating that the electrochemical discharge reaction had been
occurred on the surface of the Ag NW in this Li-O2 nanobattery.
Ag NPs with radii approximately 15 nm distributed on porous
carbon matrix were designed by using a template method, and it
reveals high catalytic performance when it was used as the catalyst
in normal Li-O2 battery. The Li-O2 battery reveals a maximum
discharge capacity of 17,371 mAh·g−1 and a high cycle stability
(100 times).
