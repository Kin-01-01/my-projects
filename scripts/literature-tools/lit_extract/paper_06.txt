Abstract
Li–CO2/O2 batteries, a promising energy storage technology, not only provide
ultrahigh discharge capacity but also capture CO2 and turn it into renewable
energy. Their electrochemical reaction pathways' ambiguity, however, creates
a hurdle for their practical application. This study used copper selenide (CuSe)
nanosheets as the air cathode medium in an environmental transmission
electron microscope to in situ study Li–CO2/O2 (mix CO2 as well as O2 at a
volume ratio of 1:1) and Li–O2 batteries as well as Li–CO2 batteries. Primary
discharge reactions take place successively in the Li–CO2/O2–CuSe nanobat-
tery: (I) 4Li+ + O2 + 4e−→2Li2O; (II) Li2O + CO2 →Li2CO3. The charge
reaction proceeded via (III) 2Li2CO3 →4Li+ + 2CO2 + O2 + 4e−. However,
Li–O2 and Li–CO2 nanobatteries showed poor cycling stability, suggesting the
difficulty in the direct decomposition of the discharge product. The
fluctuations of the Li–CO2/O2 battery's electrochemistry were also shown to
depend heavily on O2. The CuSe‐based Li–CO2/O2 battery showed exceptional
electrochemical performance. The Li–CO2/O2 battery offered a discharge
capacity apex of 15,492 mAh g−1 and stable cycling 60 times at 100 mA g−1.
Our research offers crucial insight into the electrochemical behavior of
Li–CO2/O2, Li–O2, and Li–CO2 nanobatteries, which may help the creation of
high‐performance Li–CO2/O2 batteries for energy storage applications.

INTRODUCTION
Owing to their impressive theoretical energy density that
is adequate for possible applications, Li–air batteries have
attracted interest on a global scale.1–3 Theoretically,
lithium–oxygen (Li–O2) batteries have an energy density
of 3500 Wh kg−1, while the energy density of lithium
carbon dioxide (Li–CO2) batteries stands at 1876 Wh
kg−1,4–10 a value noticeably surpassing that of analogous
lithium‐ion batteries.11,12 Extensive research into the
functioning processes of Li–CO2 batteries with the
addition of O2 and the improvement of these batteries
are required to engineer Li‐air batteries that can operate
effectively in CO2 and O2 coexisting settings.13–20
The substitution of CO2 for O2 in the cathode appears to
be a potential green energy storage strategy. In addition to
providing an energy storage solution, it also works well as a
method of CO2 sequestration, reducing emissions of green-
house gases and reducing the effects of climate change.21–25
However, the irreversibility of Li2CO3 poses a substantial
impediment to the progression of Li–CO2 batteries.26–28
Notably, Li2CO3 is an insulator and is insoluble in the
electrolyte, which unavoidably results in continual Li2CO3
accumulation and irreversible coating of active sites after the
discharge process.29,30 To properly decompose Li2CO3 with
low charge overpotential, sophisticated cathode catalysts
and advanced technology must be rationally designed.
Using a simple method, Li et al.31 introduced a covalent
organic framework (COF) serving successfully as an air
cathode in Li–CO2 batteries. The COF‐integrated Li–CO2
battery exhibits a threshold capacity of 1000 mAh g−1, a low
cut‐off overpotential of 1.24 V, and a remarkable capacity of
27,348 mAh g−1 at a current density of 200 mA g−1. The
COF‐based battery can sustain discharging/charging at a
high current density of 1000 mA g−1, maintaining operation
over 200 cycles. In their investigation of Li–CO2 batteries in
various temperature regimes, Xu et al.32 discovered that
high temperatures could hasten the battery discharge
process. The Au@TiO2/LAGP layer promoted the enhance-
ment in the sluggish kinetics of carbon dioxide reduction
reaction (CO2RR) and carbon dioxide evolution reaction
(CO2ER). By leveraging photoelectric and photothermal
effects, Guan et al.33 recorded promising results. With
illumination, the photoassisted Li–CO2 battery demonstrates
an exceptionally low polarization of 0.25 V and a high
round‐trip efficiency of 92.4%.
Many scientists are currently studying Li–air batteries
that include both CO2 and O2 because it has been shown
that adding O2 to Li–CO2 systems has a significant
impact
on
electrochemical
reactions
and
increases
energy
storage
capacity.34–38
According
to
Takechi
et al.,39 an initial low capacity of 66 mAh g−1 was
exhibited, yet the addition of 10% O2 to CO2 could
elevate the capacity to approximately 4000 mAh g−1.
They claimed that CO2 could not be directly decreased
electrochemically during the discharge process. On the
contrary, to produce a high discharge capacity, O2 was
initially reduced to the superoxide anion radical (O2
•−),
subsequently consumed rapidly by CO2. As demonstrated
by Zhang et al.,40 compared to the Li–CO2 battery
(0.44 V, 110 cycles), the Li–CO2/O2 battery displays a
negligible overpotential of 0.31 V and cycle stability of
127 cycles. Wang et al.41 proved the O2–assisted Li–CO2
battery to offer a low charge voltage of 4.02 V, a high
Coulombic efficiency of 89.2%, and an acceptable cycle
stability (67 cycles at a limited capacity of 500 mAh g−1).
Despite tremendous progress, the Li–CO2/O2 battery's
workings continue to be a mystery.
We report in situ observations of the Li–CO2/O2
nanobattery under an environmental transmission elec-
tron microscope (ETEM) in this study aiming to decipher
the role of O2 in Li–CO2 batteries and demystify the
operational mechanism of the Li–CO2/O2 nanobattery.
Real‐time revelations of the discharge/charge processes,
discharge/charge products, and electrochemical reaction
mechanisms were made.

Materials synthesis
Using a solvothermal process, CuSe samples were
produced. In a mixture of 15 mL of deionized water
and 10 mL of ethylene glycol (C2H6O2), 1 mmol of
Na2SeO3, 2 mmol of Cu(CH3COO)2·H2O, and 2 mmol of
NaOH were combined. A 30 min agitation was followed
by the transference of the mixture into a 50 mL autoclave
(equipped with a Teflon seal) for a 12 h hydrothermal
treatment at 150°C in an electric oven (see Figure 1A).
The resulting black precipitate was gathered post-
treatment via centrifugation, washed three times using
water and ethanol, and subsequently dried in a furnace.

progressive emergence of nanopores, which were fully
produced after 370 s of charging (see Figure 2D,E).
Additional nanoparticles emerged when the CuSe was
subjected to a subsequent negative potential application
(see Figure 2F,G), and nanopores reappeared when the

these observations could be replicated, this phenomenon
was

(see

experiment indicate that the Li–CO2/O2 battery's discharge
and charging dynamics are unaffected by the e‐beam.
In this paper, we used a precision‐controlled potentiostat
(PiciFemto V3ST; provided by Anhui Zeyou Technology Co.,
Ltd.) to control the applied voltage, which was the
controller's output and was the programmed voltage with
an error margin of 4.5 mV. First, either constant voltage or
constant current can be applied in electrochemical reactions.
Second, the contact resistance for probe‐type contacts is
relatively high. As the reaction changes, the contact
resistance may become unstable, and constant current is
prone to intensely heat the sample. In constant current
mode, when the contact resistance is unstable, the voltage
will fluctuate greatly, and virtual connection may directly
cause the equipment to reach the limit voltage range,
seriously compromising the safety of equipment operation.
Finally, in the study, choosing to control the constant voltage
and then limit the current can enable the reaction to proceed
normally and also avoid some drawbacks of the constant
current mode.
To evaluate the morphological and structural altera-
tions in the CuSe cathode over the discharge and charge
cycles, we used ADF imaging, electron diffraction
patterns (EDPs), and electron energy loss spectroscopy
(EELS) (see Figure 3). Figure 3A,B shows the ADF
images of the discharge and charge products, where the
discharge phase involves the transport of lithium ions
from the lithium anode to the CuSe cathode, and the
converse happens during the charging process. In the
course of discharge, the products generated numerous
white particles on the CuSe nanosheet, which appeared
as lighter contrast areas. On the other hand, the
nanosheet had many nanopores during the charging
phase. According to results from conventional lithium‐
ion batteries, the CuSe first underwent the following
conversion reaction, which led to a partial transforma-
tion into Cu: CuSe + 2Li →Cu + Li2Se, aligning with
observations from traditional lithium‐ion batteries.42–44
The final stage of the discharge process predominantly
featured Li2CO3 as the discharge product. Upon the
inversion of bias, the EDP associated with the charging
products surfaced, primarily indexed as Cu and Li2Se,
with a minor presence of CuSe (see Figure 3C,D).
According
to
the
diffraction
integral
diagram
(see
Figure 3E), the main products of discharge were Li2CO3,
Cu, Li2Se, and trace amounts of CuSe, whereas the
majority of the Li2CO3 had disintegrated after charging.
Cu and Li2Se, which are crucial metal and ionic
conductors, make it easier for lithium ions to move
between the positive and negative electrodes of the
battery. This has a substantial impact on understanding
how the cycling of the Li–CO2/O2 battery can enhance
the discharge efficiency and cycle longevity. As a result,
Li–CO2/O2 batteries' inclusion of Cu and Li2Se is
essential for maintaining consistent battery performance.

Considering the EDP and EELS results, we propose the
following sequence for the CO2RR: initial conversion
reaction leads to the transformation of CuSe into Li2Se and
Cu (see Figure 4). The newly created ultrafine‐grained Cu
then act as a catalyst in the creation of O2−from O2
molecules. Fresh O2−reacts with Li+ to create Li2O, which
then combines with CO2 to create Li2CO3. The CO2RR
becomes stronger as the amount of ultrafine−grained Cu
increases, demonstrating their catalytic involvement. The
battery's discharge operation is shown in Figure 4 with the
following detailed steps: (I) Li →Li+ + e−; (II) CuSe + 2Li+ +
2e−→Cu + Li2Se; (III) the freshly produced Cu catalyzed
reaction
4Li+ + O2 + 4e−→2Li2O;
(IV)
Li2O + CO2 →
Li2CO3. In addition, the ultrafine−grained Cu speed up
Li2CO3's partial decomposition during the CO2ER. Li2CO3
decomposes electrochemically as the CuSe develops a
positive bias against the Li counter electrode. The electro-
chemical decomposition of the Li2CO3 occurs: (V) 2Li2CO3 →
4Li+ + 2CO2 + O2 + 4e−; (VI) Li+ + e−→Li (see Figure 4).

(average size: 5.8 nm) in the transformation reaction of CuSe

CONCLUSION
In summary, the Li–CO2/O2–CuSe battery was success-
fully put through in situ discharge/charge cycles in the
ETEM, revealing in real‐time the fundamental electro-
chemical reaction processes within these nanobatteries.
During the discharge phase, Li2O was created when Li+
combined with O2 and electron to form Li2O, which then
underwent a chemical reaction with CO2 to create
Li2CO3. During the charging step, Li2CO3 was broken
down into Li, O2, and CO2 with the help of ultrafine‐
grained Cu. Additionally, it has been shown that O2 is a
critical function in the electrochemical process of the
Li–CO2/O2 battery. With a current density of 500 mA g−1,
the Li–CO2/O2 battery's maximum discharge capacity
was 5168 mAh g−1. The Li–CO2/O2 batteries demon-
strated a noteworthy performance over 60 cycles, with
steady discharge/charge voltage plateaus at a current
density of 100 mA g−1. These discoveries provide a
thorough understanding to the functioning mechanism
of the Li–CO2/O2 and Li–CO2 batteries, potentially
informing future designs for energy storage purposes.
