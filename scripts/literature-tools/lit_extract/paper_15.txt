ABSTRACT 
Rechargeable lithium-carbon dioxide (Li-CO2) batteries have attracted much attention due to their high theoretical energy densities and 
capture of CO2. However, the electrochemical reaction mechanisms of rechargeable Li-CO2 batteries, particularly the decomposition 
mechanisms of the discharge product Li2CO3 are still unclear, impeding their practical applications. Exploring electrochemistry of 
Li2CO3 is critical for improving the performance of Li-CO2 batteries. Herein, in-situ environmental transmission electron microscopy 
(ETEM) technique was used to study electrochemistry of Li2CO3 in Li-CO2 batteries during discharge and charge processes. 
During discharge, Li2CO3 was nucleated and accumulated on the surface of the cathode media such as carbon nanotubes (CNTs) and 
Ag nanowires (Ag NWs), but it was hard to decompose during charging at room temperature. To promote the decomposition of Li2CO3, 
the charge reactions were conducted at high temperatures, during which Li2CO3 was decomposed to lithium with release of gases. 
Density functional theory (DFT) calculations revealed that the synergistic effect of temperature and biasing facilitates the decomposition 
of Li2CO3. This study not only provides a fundamental understanding to the high temperature Li-CO2 nanobatteries, but also offers a valid 
technique, i.e., discharging/charging at high temperatures, to improve the cyclability of Li-CO2 batteries for energy storage applications.

in-situ environmental transmission electron microscopy, high temperature Li-CO2 nanobatteries, electrochemistry, Li2CO3 
 
1 Introduction 
Lithium-carbon dioxide (Li-CO2) batteries have attracted great 
attention in the past few years due to their high theoretical 
specific energy density (1,876 Wh·kg−1), which is much higher 
than that of lithium ion batteries (~ 265 Wh·kg−1) [1–3]. 
However, the irreversibility of Li2CO3 greatly hampers the 
development of Li-CO2 batteries [4, 5]. Notably, Li2CO3 is an 
insulator and insoluble to the electrolyte, which inevitably 
causes continuous accumulation of Li2CO3 and irreversible 
coverage of active sites after the discharge process [6, 7]. The 
accumulated Li2CO3 insulating layer separates the reaction 
intermediates from contacting with Li+/e− and requires more 
energy input to be decomposed [8, 9]. The decomposition of 
Li2CO3 requires a high potential during the charging process, 
which ultimately leads to poor cycling stability, large polarization 
and rapid electrolyte decomposition under high voltage [10]. 
Techniques such as the electrolyte additives, electrolyte redox 
mediator, electrocatalysis, biological modification, and high 
temperature are widely employed to improve the performance 
of Li-CO2 batteries [11–16].  
Plenty of catalysts are designed for improving the efficiency 
and reversibility of the discharge and charge reactions in the 
Li-CO2 batteries [17–21]. Guo et al. synthesized RuP2 nanoparticles 
highly dispersed on N, P dual-doped carbon films (RuP2-NPCFs) 
by a simple pyrolysis and explored their electrocatalytic 
properties on Li-CO2 batteries [22]. Wang et al. synthesized Ru 
nanoparticles monodispersed in functionalized graphene nano-
sheets as cathode catalyst for Li-CO2 batteries, which exhibited 
an excellent rechargeability with a low charge voltage (4.02 V), 
and a good Coulomb efficiency of 89.2% [23]. Bie et al. 
synthesized CNT@RuO2 composite as cathode catalyst for Li- 
CO2 batteries, which exhibited an excellent discharge capacity 
(2,187 mAh·g−1), a low charge voltage (3.97 V), and a high first 
cycle Coulomb efficiency (100%) [24]. Both Ru and RuO2 
nanoparticles as cathode catalysts for Li-CO2 batteries are 
important for the efficient decomposition of Li2CO3 at low 
overpotential. However, the high cost of these noble metal 
catalysts greatly hinders their widespread practical applications. 
Therefore, it is urgent to develop some catalysts with a low

Although much effort has been devoted to improving the 
performance of Li-CO2 battery [35–39], the detailed working 
mechanism during charge and discharge processes is still 
ambiguous, which greatly hinders its application. There is some 
inconsistence or even controversial conclusions regarding the 
reaction mechanisms of the Li-CO2 batteries [7, 40–42]. Some 
considered that Li2CO3 and amorphous carbon (a-C) were 
the main discharge products [17], while others reported that 
Li2CO3 and CO were the main discharge products during 
discharge [40]. There are two possible discharge mechanisms 
of Li-CO2 batteries, which are 
4Li + 3CO2 → 2Li2CO3 + C            (1) 
via four-electron transfer and  
2Li + 2CO2 → Li2CO3 + CO            (2) 
via two-electron transfer.  
Moreover, there are two different pathways reported for the 
decomposition of Li2CO3 [43]:  
2Li2CO3 + C → 4Li+ + 3CO2 + 4e− [2]           (3) 
or              2Li2CO3 → 4Li + 2CO2 + O2 [29]                    (4) 
In order to clarify the working mechanism of Li-CO2 nano-
battery. Herein, the discharge and charge reactions in a Li-CO2 
nanobattery were investigated through in-situ environmental 
transmission electron microscopy (ETEM) technique. A direct 
visualization of the Li2CO3 nucleation and decomposing process 
was realized. The electrochemical reaction mechanisms at room 
temperature and high temperatures are clarified. 
2  Experimental 
2.1  Materials synthesis 
The CNTs were prepared by electrically arcing carbon rods 
in high purity (99.99%) He atmosphere in a stainless-steel arc 
discharge chamber with an inner diameter of 165 mm and a 
height of 410 mm [44, 45]. The anode was an asphalt-derived 
carbon rod, and the cathode was a graphite electrode (purity 
99.99%, 6 mm in diameter, 38 mm in length). The buffer gas 
pressure was controlled in the 0.04–0.05 MPa range during the 
arc discharge experiment. The current and voltage of the direct 
current (DC) system for arcing were controlled at 60–80 A 
and 18–20 V, respectively. 
2.2  In-situ nanobattery setup 
The Li-CO2 nanobattery was constructed based on our previous 
all-solid-state cell setup [46–50], which consists of a Li anode, 
a Li2CO3 electrolyte, and a single CNT cathode media. More 
specifically, a tungsten (W) tip scratched metal lithium (Li) was 
used as a negative electrode, and a CNT or an Ag nanowire 
(NW) was used as the CO2 electrode. The nanobattery was 
assembled by a micro-electromechanical system-scanning 
tunneling microscopy-transmission electron microscopy 
(MEMS-STM-TEM) specimen holder (Pico Femto TMH4-ST) 
inside a glove box. Then the holder was sealed in a home-built 
air-tight bag filled with dry Ar gas and transferred to the 
ETEM (FEI, Titan G2, 300 kV) chamber. The total exposure 
time to the air was less than 2 s, which limited the extent of 
Li2O formation on the surface of the Li metal. During the 
discharge/charge experiments, CO2 gas (purity higher than 
99.99%) with a pressure of 1 mbar was flowed into the ETEM 
chamber. The electrochemical reaction was controlled by 
external biasing and heating. When a bias was applied to the 
CNT or Ag NW against the Li counter electrode, discharge/charge 
reaction took place, leading to the formation/decomposition 
of Li2CO3.  
2.3  Theoretical calculation 
All calculations were performed in a Gaussian09 suite of 
quantum programs, and Simulation of Arrhenius Equation in 
COMSOL. Local minimum structures were optimized using 
the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) based hybrid 
functional of B3LYP combined with the all electron basis sets and 
polarization functions of 6-31G(d) [51–54]. The vibrational 
frequencies were calculated at the same theoretical level, ensuring 
that the reactant and product structures were located at a 
true minimum on the potential energy surface (PES), and 
the transition state was a first-order saddle point on PES as 
well. The partition function was computed through frequency 
calculations. 
3  Results and discussion 
Figure 1(a) shows a photograph of the TEM-STM holder with 
a MEMS heating device, which enables biasing and heating 
at the same time (Figs. 1(b) and 1(c)) [55]. Figure 1(d) shows a 
TEM image of a nanobattery, where the CNT cathode media is 
connected to the Li metal anode. During experiments, 1 mbar 
CO2 (purity higher than 99.99%) gas was continuously injected 
into the ETEM chamber. 
Morphology and structure evolution of the CNT cathode 
during discharge and charge were analyzed by TEM images, 
electron diffraction patterns (EDPs) and electron energy loss 
spectroscopy (EELS) (Figs. 2(a)–2(n) and Movie ESM1). Part 
of the pristine CNT at the right end was inserted under the 
metal lithium during making the contact (Figs. 2(a) and 2(b)), and 
during discharge the segment that hiding below the lithium 
metal appeared to be pulled out due to the electrochemical 
stress, which gave an erroneous visual effect that the CNT 
was elongated. To confirm this conclusion, we measured the 
length of the CNT before and after cycling. The actual length 
of the pristine CNT was 933 nm (Fig. 2(a)). After discharge for 
1,682 s, the length was 938 nm (Fig. 2(e)). Considering there 
is possibly some Li2CO3 deposition at the right end of the CNT, 
we conclude that no length change of the CNT has occurred 
during discharging and charging. After discharge for 971 s at 
room temperature, the surface of the CNT was coated with a 
large amount of gray material and the CNT bent (Figs. 2(b)– 
2(d)). The CNT used in this study is highly graphitized. Its 
layered structure provides stable hosts for the Li+ inserting 
into the interior. So there is Li+ insertion into the layers of the 
CNT during discharge [56–58]. The diameters of CNT expanded 
~ 225%, resulting in a total volume expansion of about 914% 
(Fig. 2(e)). EDPs (Figs. 2(j) and 2(k)) and EELS (Figs. 2(m) 
and 2(n)) analysis indicates that the newly generated substances 
were mainly Li2CO3. After discharge, the nanobattery was 
recharged by applying a reverse bias of +4 V. Although applying 
a quite high voltage (+4 V) and charged for a period of time 
(458 s), negligible change of the discharge product was observed, 
indicating that the decomposition of the discharge products 
was difficult during the charge process (Fig. 2(f) and Fig. S1 in 
the Electronic Supplementary Material (ESM)).  
We conducted a further analysis of the Li substrate. It is 
found that the surface of the Li substrate was coated by a thick 
layer of Li2CO3 with a thickness of more than ~ 100 nm (Fig. S2 
in the ESM). And the CNT cathode formed a point contact

How to promote the decomposition of Li2CO3 is still a critical 
issue for the Li-CO2 battery. Das et al. found that increasing the 
temperature could significantly speed up the ion migration 
speed and reaction rate, achieving a fast charging of lithium 
ion battery [16]. Our previous in-situ study indicates that 
high temperature facilitates the charge reaction of the Li-S 
nanobattery and the Na-O2 nanobattery dramatically [55, 59]. 
Enhancing the temperature is a good choice for improving 
the performance of lithium batteries [1, 16]. Inspired by these 
previous studies, we heated up the discharge products up to 
300 °C for promoting the charge reaction (Figs. 2(g)–2(i)). By 
applying a bias voltage of +4 V at 300 °C, plenty of tiny bubbles 
emerged on the surfaces of the CNT (Fig. 2(g) and Fig. S3 in 
the ESM), indicating initiation of decomposition of Li2CO3 
and release of gas at 300 C. At the end of the charging reaction 
(5,253 s), the CNT almost returned to its original shape 
(Fig. 2(i)). The final products after decomposition were a-C 
(Fig. 2(l)). In order to prove that the decomposition of Li2CO3 
was electrochemically driven rather than thermal decomposition 
caused by high temperature, we conducted a growth and 
decomposition experiment of Li2CO3 at 300 °C (Fig. S4 in the 
ESM). It is seen that Li2CO3 was formed during discharge, and 
decomposed during charge at 300 °C, demonstrating a good 
cyclability at high temperatures, which is in sharp contrast to 
the room temperature experiments where the charge reaction 
could not proceed. We found that the electrochemically grown 
Li2CO3 could not be decomposed by heating alone below 
500 °C (Fig. S5 in the ESM). Thermal decomposition was 
achieved at temperatures above 500 °C. Meanwhile, we also 
conducted a separate heating experiment for commercial 
Li2CO3 powder, which further confirmed this conclusion (Fig. S6 
in the ESM). The decomposition of Li2CO3 powder started 
from 500 °C, and completed at 750 °C [60, 61]. These results 
indicate that it is the synergistic effect of high temperature 
and charge potential, rather than heating or biasing alone, that 
drives the decomposition of Li2CO3 at temperatures below 500 °C. 
Regarding the decomposition or melting point difference in 
vacuum and in atmosphere, a previous study shows that the 
decomposition temperature is lower in a vacuum environment 
than in atmosphere [62]. We performed in situ heating 
experiment of Li2CO3 in the vacuum of TEM, and found that 
the decomposition temperature was 500 °C, which was lower 
than that in atmosphere. 
It is well known that high temperature could accelerate the 
electrochemical reaction in general, however, the dynamics 
regarding how the temperature can boost the Li-CO2 reaction 
has not been revealed before. Such studies can help to 
understand the reaction kinetics and the mechanisms of the 
Li-CO2 battery, which are still under debate. For example, at 
room temperature, the discharge product Li2CO3 is very difficult 
to decompose under our experimental condition, rendering 
in-situ study of the charge reaction mechanism of Li-CO2 nano-
battery very difficult. However, by raising up the temperature 
during charge, the decomposition of Li2CO3 was achieved, 
thus enabling in-situ study of the reaction mechanism of the 
Li-CO2 nanobattery. 
Regarding the influence of CO2 pressures on the reaction 
pathway, we conducted the charging and discharging experiments 
by varying the gas pressures. It is found that the reaction 
phenomenon and byproduct were almost the same under 
different pressures from 0.1 to 8.0 mbar (Figs. S7 and S8 in the 
ESM). The discharge reaction is 2Li + 2CO2 → Li2CO3 + C, and 
the charge reaction is 2Li2CO3 → 4Li + 2CO2 + O2. As shown 
in Table S1 in the ESM, we also summarized recent articles 
about the reaction mechanisms of Li-CO2 battery [2, 10, 11, 22, 
27, 30, 35, 43]. Our results are consistent with those reported 
in Refs. [11, 43]. 
The discharge/charge products were further analyzed by 
EELS (Figs. 2(m) and 2(n)). The plasmon loss peak of the pristine 
CNT was 24.1 eV (Fig. 2(m), green line), but the peak located 
at 20.9 eV was collected from the discharge product of Li2CO3 
only (Fig. 2(m), red line), as marked by a red spot in Fig. 2(e). 
The three different colored dots in Fig. 2 represent the positions 
where EELS was acquired. The green, red and blue dots 
represent the original CNT, discharge and charge product, 
respectively (Figs. 2(b), 2(e) and 2(i)). After discharge, the 
surface of CNT was covered by a thick layer of Li2CO3, and the 
EELS was acquired from only the surface Li2CO3 thus no carbon 
peak was observed (Figs. 2(e) and 2(m), red). Meanwhile, the 
Li-K (Fig. 2(m), red), O-K and C-K edges (Fig. 2(n), red line) 
appeared in the core-loss spectra, which confirmed the formation 
of Li2CO3 on the surface of the CNT after discharge process. 
The major plasmon loss peak of the charged products also 
shifted from 20.9 eV (Fig. 2(m), red line) of Li2CO3 to 24.1 eV 
of carbon (Fig. 2(m), blue line), suggesting the presence of a-C. 
In the core-loss of the charge reaction products, it showed 
the presence of a-C (Fig. 2(n), blue line). Combining with the 
EDP results, the residue on the surface of CNT after charge 
was a-C.  
We found that the discharged product was Li2CO3, which 
was decomposed into carbon after charge process (Fig. S9 in the 
ESM). The high resolution transmission electron microscopy 
(HRTEM) images can supplementarily prove the conclusion 
about the discharging and charging products and electro-
chemical mechanism.  
Being as an insoluble wide band gap insulator, Li2CO3 is 
difficult to decompose electrochemically, leading to sluggish 
kinetics for the charge reaction of Li-CO2 batteries. Consequently, 
the accumulation of insoluble Li2CO3 on the cathode surface 
blocked the pathway for CO2 and Li+ diffusion and eventually 
shut down the electrochemical reactions of the Li-CO2 
batteries. 
Our results indicate that high temperature greatly enhanced 
the cyclability of the Li-CO2 batteries (Figs. 2 and 3, and Fig. S3 
in the ESM and Movie ESM2). Figure 3 shows multiple cycling of 
a Li-CO2 nanobattery. The discharge reactions were conducted 
at room temperature (Figs. 3(a), 3(b); 3(e), 3(f); 3(i), 3(j)), 
while the charge reaction was performed at 300 °C (Figs. 3(c), 
3(d); 3(g), 3(h); 3(k), 3(l)). It is noted that with increasing 
number of cycles, a-C residue became thicker after each cycle 
(Figs. 3(d), 3(h) and 3(l)), which could be a problem for the 
cyclability of Li-CO2 nanobatteries, as the accumulated thicker 
carbon layer may slow down the Li+ ion diffusion and the 
electrochemical reaction.  
In this study, we found uniform decomposition of Li2CO3 in 
the radical direction during the charge process at 300 °C. As 
shown in Fig. S10 in the ESM, uniform bubble-like pores 
were observed from the right to the left, suggesting that the 
decomposition of Li2CO3 proceeds from the right to the left. 
This is caused by the special experimental configuration of the 
nanobattery. Namely, the CNT forms a point contact with the 
Li2CO3 solid state electrolyte (SSE) on the right, rendering a 
one-dimensional (1D) ion transport; because the right end 
of the CNT is close to the SSE, thus Li+ ions are first extracted 
from the Li2CO3 on the right end of the CNT, thus the right to 
left decomposition of Li2CO3. Similar phenomenon has been 
observed in 1D nanowire nanobatteries [63].

routes (Reactions (1) and (2)) and two possible charge reaction 
pathways (Reactions (3) and (4)) as well. In-situ TEM revealed 
the formation of a-C in the discharge products (Figs. 2(a)–2(d) 
and Figs. S3(a)–S3(c) in the ESM), and therefore, the discharge

production of a-C due to the contamination of the TEM 
chamber. However, in this experiment, we found almost no 
obvious carbon deposition after irradiating the CNT for more 
than two hours under the similar experimental conditions to the 
Li-CO2 nanobattery operation (Fig. S11 in the ESM). Therefore, 
the discharge product carbon was caused by electrochemical

irradiation. 
CNTs have been extensively utilized as the cathode materials 
in the rechargeable Li-CO2 batteries mainly because of their 
adequate electrical conductivity, large surface area and low cost 
[9, 32, 64]. In our experiment, CNT was a good electronic con-
ductor and ion conductor, and it exhibited a good performance 
as the electrode in the reaction 4Li + 3CO2 → 2Li2CO3 + C. In 
order to explore the reaction mechanism and avoid the carbon 
influence from the CNT electrode, we also used Ag NW as the 
CO2 cathode for the discharge/charge experiments. This method 
has been widely applied to verify the reaction mechanism in 
metal-CO2 batteries [38, 65]. The morphology and structure 
evolutions of the discharge and charge products on the Ag NW 
cathode are shown in Figs. 4(a)–4(j). As shown in Figs. 4(a) 
and 4(d), the pristine Ag NW was single crystal (JCPDS card 
No. 65-2871) with the growth direction of [111]. Upon applying 
a negative potential (−4 V) to the Ag NW cathode against the 
Li anode for 3,665 s, a thin layer of Li2CO3 formed (Figs. 4(b) 
and 4(e)). When the voltage was reversed, plenty of nano balls 
emerged in the Li2CO3 layer after charging for 5,192 s at 500 °C 
(Fig. 4(c)). The EDP shows the diffraction rings of carbon 
(Fig. 4(f)), indicating that Li2CO3 had been totally decomposed 
and the residual substance was a-C (Figs. 4(c) and 4(f)). The 
morphology and structural evolutions of another Ag NW during 
discharging and charging in a CO2 environment further con-
firmed this conclusion (Fig. S12 in the ESM and Movie ESM3). 
The discharge/charge products were analyzed by EELS. For the 
Li2CO3 generated in discharge, it displayed a major plasmon 
peak resided at 20.9 eV with a shoulder peak resided at 13.4, 
58.9 and 63.8 eV (Fig. 4(g), red line). Compared with the 
commercial Li2CO3 EELS result (Fig. S13 in the ESM), we 
assign these four characteristic peaks to Li2CO3. In addition, the 
core-loss spectrum shows the presence O-K and C-K edges 
(red line in Fig. 4(h)), confirming the formation of Li2CO3 
during the discharge process. The low-loss spectrum of the 
partially charged product was different from that of the deeply 
discharged one, and the former shows a major low-loss peak at 
24.2 eV (Fig. 4(g), blue line). The core-loss spectrum from the 
discharge product shows the presence of C and O, and that after 
charge shows the presence of a-C, indicating the formation 
of Li2CO3 during discharge and the decomposition of Li2CO3 
with the residue of a-C during charge at 500 °C. Meanwhile, we 
analyzed the discharge/charge products further by HRTEM, 
indicating that the discharged products were Li2CO3 and a-C 
(Fig. 4(i)), while the charged products were mainly a-C (Fig. 4(j)). 
These results further reinforce our conclusion that the discharge 
reaction took place via Reaction (1) while the charge reaction 
occurred via Reaction (4). From Reaction (1), the amount of 
Li2CO3 should be larger than that of carbon. However, as 
shown in the diffraction pattern (Fig. 2(k)), the discharged 
product of Li2CO3 displayed a poor crystallinity, although small 
amount of nanocrystalline Li2CO3 was also formed. It is hard 
to differentiate amorphous Li2CO3 from a-C in the HRTEM 
image. Moreover, the HRTEM image was acquired from a 
very local region, which may not represent the entire sample. 
Therefore we were unable to calculate the ratio of Li2CO3 from 
the HRTEM images. 
Temperature plays a significant role for the decomposition 
of Li2CO3. We explored the decomposing behaviors of Li2CO3 
at a series of temperatures lower than 300 °C (Fig. S14 in the 
ESM). It is found that Li2CO3 could also be decomposed below 
300 °C, but the decomposition rate became quite slow. At 100 °C, 
the decomposition rate of Li2CO3 reduced to 0.09 nm·min−1, 
which was 0.38 nm·min−1 at 300 °C. The higher the temperature, 
the faster the decomposing rate. When using the Ag NW as 
cathode, it is found that the decomposition rate of Li2CO3 
was quite slow at 300 °C, so we selected 500 °C as its reaction 
temperature (Fig. S15 in the ESM).

one order of magnitude higher than that at room temperature.

position kinetics of Li2CO3. 
4  Conclusions 
In summary, we successfully conducted in-situ discharging/ 
charging of the Li-CO2 nanobattery at high temperatures 
inside an ETEM. During discharge, a thick layer of Li2CO3 was 
quickly formed on the CNT cathode via 4Li+ + 4e− + 3CO2 → 
2Li2CO3 + C. However, the Li2CO3 was hard to decompose 
during the following charge process at room temperature. 
When charged at 300 °C, Li2CO3 began to quickly decompose 
via the reaction of 2Li2CO3 → 4Li + 2CO2 + O2. DFT calculations 
revealed that the synergistic effect of temperature and applied 
voltage contributed to the enhanced Li2CO3 decomposition. 
Our results not only provide an in-depth understanding to the 
fundamental electrochemistry of Li-CO2 batteries, but also offer 
a strategy to improve their cycling performance, which may 
have important implications for the design of Li-CO2 batteries 
for energy storage applications.
