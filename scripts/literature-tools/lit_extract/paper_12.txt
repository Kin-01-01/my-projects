a b s t r a c t
Metal nanoparticles have important applications in batteries, fuel cells, electrocatalysis, and sensing.
However, their growth mechanisms are still unclear. Here the growth mechanism of Cu nanoparticles
during electrochemical deposition was characterized via an in situ transmission electron microscopy
observation in an open ionic liquid cell. We revealed a hierarchical agglomeration growth (HAG)
mechanism, namely, single Cu atoms were formed ﬁrst due to electrochemical reduction of Cu2þ ions.
The single Cu atoms then agglomerated into nanoclusters, which subsequently recrystallized into small
nanocrystals. The small nanocrystals agglomerated into larger nanocrystals, and then formed a porous
nanocrystals network with an average grain size of 10 nm. The large Cu nanoparticles are single crystals
with numerous multiple twins. A self-limiting grain growth mechanism governed by the Cu2þ ion
limited diffusion was also found. Our study demonstrates the possibility of observing nanoparticle
growth under realistic electrochemical conditions, which may have important applications in tailoring
the nanoparticle size and shape for practical applications.

1. Introduction
Metal nanocrystals possess unique properties that differ from
their bulk counterparts and can be tuned by adjusting their size and
shape [1,2]. They have important applications in ﬁelds such as
catalysis batteries [2,3], fuel cells [4,5], FischereTropsch synthesis
[6,7] and sensing [8,9]. They are mainly synthesized by solution-
based methods or electrochemical deposition (ECD) [10e12]. Un-
derstanding the growth mechanism of metal nanocrystals is critical
to tailor
their
size
and
morphology
for
the
corresponding
applications. However, despite the extensive research, the growth
mechanisms of nanocrystals are still unclear.
The prevalent metal nanocrystal growth mechanism is based on
the well-known VolmereWeber 3D island growth mechanism in
which nanocrystal grows radially by direct atom attachment to
spherical nuclei [12]. Others have invoked the LaMer mechanism
[13e19], Ostwald ripening [20e33], or their combination [34e37].
The main feature of the LaMer mechanism is that nucleation occurs
at an early burst period. In Ostwald ripening, larger nanocrystals
grow at the expense of smaller nanocrystals [38]. Very recently an
aggregative growth mechanism (AGM) was proposed. AGM sug-
gests that nanocluster nucleation and growth, aggregation and
coalescence are the main mechanisms of early nanocrystal growth.
AGM is initiated from the nucleation of primary nanoclusters that
grow until certain self-limiting growth mechanism stabilizes them
at a given size. Then, the primary nanoclusters aggregate due to
surface tension. Finally, the aggregated nanoclusters form either a

be noted that most in situ TEM studies of nanocrystal growth were
performed under electron beam stimuli, nanocrystal growth under
electrochemical stimuli is still very challenging. Huang et al.
developed an open cell electrochemical device that is compatible
with TEM, enabling real time investigation lithium-ion battery
studies [51]. In this report, we demonstrate for the ﬁrst time that in
situ electrochemical deposition can be conducted using an open cell
based on ionic liquid (IL) electrolyte. IL has very low vapor pressure,

still ﬂow like a liquid, thus permitting in situ studies of liquid-based
experiments in the high vacuum of TEM. We demonstrate that the
electrochemical deposition of Cu nanocrystals under electro-
chemical driving force features an atom attachment at the initial
stage and AGM at the second stage for the nanoparticle growth.
2. Experimental section
2.1. Materials
1-butyl-1-methylpyrrolidinium
bis
(triﬂuoromethylsulfonyl)
imide (P14TFSI) ionic liquid electrolyte (> 98.0%) and Cu bis (tri-
ﬂuoromethylsulfonyl) imide (Cu(TFSI)2) salt (96.0%) were pur-
chased from Tokyo Chemical Industry (TCI).
2.2. TEM specimen preparation
Pt pillar for in situ TEM observation was prepared by a dual-
beam focused ion beam (FIB) system (Helios G4 CX, Thermo
Fisher Scientiﬁc) operating at 2e30 kV. A standard lift-out pro-
cedure was used to directly prepare thin slices from Pt rod, then
glued the slices to a half Cu grid and milled them to a pillar with
diameter of 20e100 nm for in situ experiments.
2.3. In situ TEM deposition assembly
Our experiments were conducted in an environmental trans-
mission electron microscope (ETEM) (FEI, Titan G2, 300 kV)
equipped
with
a
transmission
electron
microscopy-scanning
tunneling microscopy (TEM-STM) holder (Pico Femto FE-F20). A
sharp Pt pillar welded on a half Cu grid was mounted on one end of
the TEM-STM holder and used as the working electrode. A Pt metal
rod was attached to the piezo manipulator in the TEM-STM holder
and used as the counter electrode. The IL electrolyte (Cu(TFSI)2 salt
dissolving into P14TFSI) on the tip of the Pt metal rod was used as
the electrolyte. In situ observation of electrochemical reactions in
liquid environment in a TEM is still challenging due to the in-
compatibility of liquid electrolyte with the high vacuum of TEM. In
other words, one cannot put a liquid electrolyte, which is one of the
essential components of an electrochemical cell, into the high
vacuum of a TEM. We discovered in our previous work that an ionic
liquid such as P14TFSI can be placed into the high vacuum
(~105 Pa) of a TEM directly with little to no evaporation, rending
an “open” electrochemical cell a possibility [51]. We demonstrate
that it is possible to conduct in situ electrochemical plating and
stripping by using an open IL cell as well. In this case, P14TFSI is the
solvent and Cu(TFSI)2 is the salt. When the Pt pillar insert into the IL
electrolyte, an external bias was supplied for the observation of Cu
nanoparticle deposition.
3. Results and discussion
Our experiments were conducted in an ETEM equipped with a
STM probe. A sharp Pt pillar welded on a half Cu grid was inserted
into one end of the TEM-STM holder and used as the working
electrode. Cu(TFSI)2 salt dissolving into P14TFSI was used as the
electrolyte. The IL electrolyte was placed on the tip of a Pt metal rod,
which was attached to the piezo manipulator in the TEM-STM
holder and used as the counter electrode (Fig. 1a). In the TEM, the
Pt metal rod with IL electrolyte was manipulated to contact the Pt
pillar electrode, thus completing the electrochemical device con-
struction. Upon applying a negative potential to the Pt pillar, elec-
trochemical Cu deposition was initiated (Fig. 1, Movie S1). Selected
area electron diffraction (SAED, Fig. S1d) and electron energy loss
spectroscopy (EELS, Fig. S1b, c) conﬁrms that the deposited product
is metallic Cu.

nanoclusters, thus the bright spots should be Cu atoms rather than
Cu ions. In addition to single atoms, small clusters (d < 5 nm) with

Fig. 3 is a schematic showing the hierarchical agglomeration
growth (HAG) mechanism of Cu nanoparticles during electro-
chemical deposition. Upon applying a negative potential to the Pt
pillar against the Pt rod, Cu2þ ions near the working electrode are
reduced to single Cu atoms, which are randomly distributed in the
IL electrolyte (step 1, Fig. 3a). Once the concentration of the Cu
atoms are supersaturated, the atoms start to aggregate into small
clusters (step 2, Fig. 3b). These clusters grow and the concentration
of Cu2þ ions in the IL electrolyte drops. When the concentration of
the Cu2þ ions drop below the level of supersaturation, no additional
nucleation events will occur, causing the self-limited nucleation.
Once formed, the clusters imping on each other and coalesce to
form larger polycrystals or monocrystals with twinned defects
(steps 3 and 4, Fig. 3c). We propose this mechanism based on our
observation that when the grain size is less than about 3 nm, twins
were rarely observed (Fig. S10). However, multiple twins or ﬁve-
fold twins were frequently observed in grains with size above
5 nm (Fig. 2ceh). Therefore, we infer that these twins are formed
during the agglomeration process. The above process repeats so
that small grains agglomerate into large ones, and the large one
further agglomerate and coalescence with each other to form larger
ones, a mechanism similar to the AGM (step 5, Fig. 3d). The
agglomeration mechanism is thermodynamic driven by the surface
energy minimization. Single atoms and smaller grains have a high
surface energy, which tend to agglomerate to large grains to
minimize the surface energy. Aggregation and coalescence of
clusters continue until all the surrounding Cu clusters are exhaus-
ted, thus limiting the grain size to a certain value. Noticeably,
during the whole deposition process, new nanoparticles are
deposited spherically layer by layer, forming a porous inter-
connected nanostructures rather than a single crystal via contin-
uous growth mechanism.

The formation of Cu nanoclusters in the IL was directly captured by

This aggregation and coalescence of nanoparticles are governed
by van der Waals attraction and electrostatic repulsion. The
attractive interactions that promote aggregation are van der Waals
forces, and the repulsive interactions are electrostatic or other
interparticle repulsions [38]. We believe that an attraction force
should exist between the Cu clusters or nanoparticles during
agglomeration in our electrochemical deposition process, however,
clusters or nanoparticles movement in this systems must be driven
by the application of an electrochemical potential [12]. The
attractive/repulsive forces determine the kinetics of the agglom-
eration. However, the ﬁnal shape and size of the nanoparticle are
controlled by the surface energy minimization.
Regarding the reason for the formation of a porous structure
rather than a single crystal, we speculate that it is related to the
existence of an ion depletion layer between the deposition elec-
trode and the IL electrolyte, which renders a nucleation controlled
rather than a growth controlled electrochemical deposition. In the
depletion layer, the concentration of Cu ions along the surface of
the agglomerated big particle (essentially the electrode surface)
drops below the level of supersaturation, therefore, continuous
deposition along the electrode surface is impossible due to the lack
of sufﬁcient Cu ions, thus precluding the single crystal formation.
Instead, nanoclusters are nucleated near the outer surface of the
depletion zone, which then drifted towards the electrode surface,
and coalescence with the bigger particles. Once the surrounding Cu
clusters are exhausted, the growth of the nanocluster then ceases,
thus limiting the grain size to a certain value. It appears that the ion
depletion layer renders a nucleation controlled rather than growth
controlled electrochemical deposition, thus the formation of
porous structure rather than a single crystal.

ionic conductivity of the IL caused by the solidiﬁcation of the IL
electrolyte at high dose electron beam irradiation. Based on these
observations, our in situ experiments were conducted at low
exposure time, and the IL was not solidiﬁed during the imaging
process, thus the inﬂuence of electron dose is negligible in our
experiments.
It should be noted that porous nanometal structures are
important catalysts for fuel cells and FischereTropsch process that
converts a mixture of carbon monoxide and hydrogen or water gas
into liquid hydrocarbons [6,7]. Traditionally these porous nano-
metal structures are synthesized by etching of metal alloys [2,10].
The electrochemical procedure offers an alternative approach for
the synthesis of such porous nanometal structures for energy
conversion applications.
4. Conclusions
We achieved a real time observation of the Cu nanoparticle
growth under electrochemical conditions using an ionic liquid open
cell inside the high vacuum of TEM. We revealed a hierarchical
grain growth mechanism characterized by the agglomeration of
smaller structures such as single atoms to form larger nano-
structures such as nanoclusters; and the nanocluster coalescence to
form larger nanocrystals, which then further agglomerated into
even larger nanocrystals. Due to diffusion controlled self-limited
growth, the nanocrystals formed an interconnected porous struc-
ture, which may have important applications in energy conversion
and energy storage ﬁelds.
