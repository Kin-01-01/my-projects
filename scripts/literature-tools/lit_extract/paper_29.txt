Abstract 
Carbon dioxide (CO2) as a greenhouse gas causes globe warming. Recent studies have 

=== PAGE 3 ===
2 
 
shown that CO2 can be harnessed to make an energy storage device through the 
operation of metal-CO2 batteries. While the Li-CO2 and Na-CO2 batteries showing 
high capacity and good cycle life have been demonstrated, the possibility of creating a 
K-CO2 battery has not been realized. Here we report the creation of a K-CO2 battery 
by using CO2 as the cathode in an advanced aberration corrected environmental 
transmission electron microscope, and in-situ observation of the discharge and charge 
processes of the nanobattery. During discharge, K2CO3 was generated in the cathode, 
which converted back to metal K during charge, and the K-CO2 nanobattery 
demonstrates a good cyclability. Moreover, it is evident that CO gas was released 
during discharge and the carbon cathode was consumed continuously during charge. 
Our study provides a fundamental understanding of the K-CO2 battery, which may aid 
the designing of K-CO2 based energy storage devices.

Introduction 
To meet the ever increasing energy demands and resolve the global warming 
environmental crisis, the development and utilization of new energy source have 
aroused great interests. In the varieties of new energy technologies, metal air batteries 
evolve rapidly owning to their zero pollution and ultra-high theoretical capacity [1-4]. 

=== PAGE 4 ===
3 
 
The theoretical energy density for Li-O2 and Na-O2 batteries is 3500 Wh/kg and 1600 
Wh/kg, respectively, which is much higher than that of the traditional Li-ion or 
Na-ion batteries (~200 Wh/kg), showing great potential for energy storage 
applications [5-9]. In most cases, metal-air batteries (such as Li-air) refer to the 
batteries working in pure oxygen conditions rather than in the air [6,10]. However, 
sometimes the oxygen may be mixed with a little CO2, leading to a quite different 
performance [11,12]. Consequently, the main discharge products become Li2CO3 
instead of Li2O2 or LiO2 [13]. Surprisingly, Archer et al. fabricated a metal-CO2 
battery, and found that its efficiency was even higher than that of Li-O2 battery [5]. 
Hu et al. created quasi-solid state rechargeable Na-CO2 batteries with reduced 
graphene oxide Na anodes [14], and found that the batteries could be cycled 
successfully for 400 cycles with a fixed capacity of 1000 mAh/g in pure CO2. 
Obviously, the research of metal-CO2 battery has aroused much attention recently 
[15].  
Till now, most literatures related to metal-CO2 batteries used metal Li or Na as 
anodes [5,13,16], and the performance of K-CO2 battery has been rarely reported. The 
element K content in the earth’s crust is about 880 times higher than Li, indicating a 
much lower price of K than that of Li [17]. Compared with Na, the standard potential 
of K/K+ couple (−2.92 V vs SHE) is lower than that of Na/Na+ (−2.71 V vs SHE), 
suggesting a higher cell voltage for K anode based battery [18]. Additionally, since 
K+ has a weaker Lewis acidity than Li+ and Na+, it possesses a faster rate of migration 
in the electrolyte and the interface between electrolyte and electrode, leading to a 
better performance of the battery. Although the K-CO2 battery shows great potential 
as future energy storage device, a proof of concept for a K-CO2 battery has not been 
realized. Herein, we report in-situ studies of a K-CO2 battery in an aberration 
corrected environmental transmission electron microscope (AC-ETEM). Carbon 
nanotubes (CNTs) were used as the counter electrode. The charging, discharging 
processes, the structure and morphology of the discharge products, and the 
electrochemical reaction mechanisms were revealed in real time. 
Experimental section  
The CNTs were prepared by electrically arcing carbon rods in high-purity 
(99.999%) N2, He and Ar atmosphere in a stainless steel arc discharge chamber with 
an inner diameter of 165 mm and a height of 410 mm. The anode was an 

=== PAGE 5 ===
4 
 
asphalt-derived carbon rod, and the cathode was a high-purity graphite electrode (20 
mm in diameter, 38 mm in length). In order to facilitate the collection of CNTs and 
improve the purity of the CNT samples, we placed a wire net on the top of the two 
electrodes in the chamber, and the distance between the wire net and the electrodes 
was about 5 cm. The buffer gas pressure was controlled in the 0.04-0.05 MPa range 
during the arc discharge experiment. The DC voltage and current for arcing were 
controlled at 18-20 V and 60-80 A, respectively. The distance between the two 
electrodes was maintained at about 1-3 mm by manually advancing the anode that 
was consumed during the experiment. The weight of the graphite cathode remained 
the same before and after the arcing experiments. During the arc discharge process, 
the pitch outflow to the open end of carbon rods were immediately evaporated due to 
the high temperature of arc discharge. After the reaction was finished, we could not 
find any asphalt in the reactor. The weight of each original hollow carbon rod, the 
filled carbon rod and the carbon rod left after reaction were about 5.0 g, 7.1 g and 4.8 
g, respectively. Only very small amount of hollow carbon rods was evaporated, 
namely, the main consumed carbon source was pitch for each kind of CNT growth. 
We collected the film-like samples from the wire net and examined them using 
scanning electron microscope.  
The nanobattery was constructed based on our previous all solid-state cell, which 
consists of a K anode, a K2O electrolyte, and a single nanotube or nanowire as the 
medium for the discharge and the charge reaction to occur. More specifically, a 
tungsten (W) tip scratched metal potassium (K) was used as a negative electrode, and 
a carbon nanotube (CNT) or a silver (Ag) nanowire was used as a medium for the 
discharge/charge reaction to occur. A pure gas of CO2 was flowed into the chamber 
with a pressure of 1 mbar during the in-situ experiments. The electrochemical reaction 
was controlled by external biasing. When a negative bias was applied to the CNT or 
Ag nanowire against the K counter electrode, discharge reaction took place, leading to 
the formation of K2CO3 balls; when a positive potential was applied to the CNT or Ag 
nanowire against the K negative electrode, charge reaction occurred, leading to the 
decomposition of the K2CO3 balls.  
Result and discussion 
Morphology evolution of the discharge/charge products 

=== PAGE 6 ===
5 
 
A schematic of an in-situ K-CO2 nanobattery setup is shown in Fig. 1a. Metal K 
serving as a counter electrode was scratched by electrochemically shaped tungsten (W) 
tips in an argon filled glove box. The naturally grown K2O on the K metal served as a 
solid electrolyte, and its thickness is about 20-50 nm (Fig. S1). The CNTs were 
attached to another Al rod with conductive silver epoxy, and the metal K and CNTs 
were incorporated into a TEM-Scanning Tunneling Microscopy (STM) holder for 
assembling the nanobattery. The detailed preparation procedure of CNTs and the 
assembling method of K-CO2 nanobattery are described in detail in the method 
section.  
Upon applying a negative potential to the CNT against the counter electrode K 
(which was grounded in our experiment), a nano ball emerged at the CNT, K2O and 
CO2 triple point, which grew quickly like blowing a balloon with lapse of time (Fig. 
1b and Movies S1-2). The ball had a thick shell and a light contrast in the core, 
suggesting a hollow structure of the ball. After 22 s, the ball inflated to a diameter of 
174 nm (Fig. 1b). Frequently the CNT was pushed away from the contact point (Fig. 
S2) while the ball continued its inflation, indicating that the generated ball can 
transport electrons and ions. When the voltage was reversed, the ball shrank 
continuously like venting of a balloon until its disappearance, and the shell became 
thicker and thicker with shrinking of the ball, which eventually decomposed entirely 
after 10 s of charge (Fig. 1c, S3). Upon applying a negative bias on the CNT again, a 
new ball appeared again at the same location where the first ball grew (Fig. 1d), and 
the ball shrank again at a reversed bias (Fig. 1e). The discharge/charge processes were 
repeatable for multiple cycles (Fig. 1f-k).  
In order to minimize the e-beam irradiation effect, we performed time-resolved 
experiments under the annular dark-field (ADF) mode to characterize the morphology 
evolution of the K-CO2 nanobatteries. In the ADF mode, the e-beam effect is 
minimized, as we only took images intermittently during the electrochemical reaction, 
which is in contrast to the bright field image mode, in which the e-beam showered the 
samples continuously. The ADF images shown in Fig. S4 demonstrate a similar 
morphology evolution process to that occurred in the bright field images (a hollow 
ball was generated in discharge and decomposed in charge, see e.g. Fig. 1), which 
confirms that the e-beam is not interfering the results and conclusions. 
Structural characterization by electron diffraction pattern (EDP) 

=== PAGE 7 ===
6 
 
The crystalline structure of the ball during discharge and charge was monitored by 
EDPs in a K-CO2 battery (Fig. 2a-h). The morphology of the pristine CNT, 
discharged, partially charged and completely charged ones are given in Fig. 2a-d, 
respectively, and their corresponding EDPs are shown in Fig. 2e-h, respectively. 
Diffraction rings ascribed to CNT were observed in the EDP of the pristine CNT (Fig. 
2e). The EDP of the ball-like discharge product (Fig. 2b) shows a series of rings 
indexed as the hexagonal K2CO3 (Fig. 2f), indicating the formation of K2CO3 during 
the discharge process. A line scan (marked by a straight yellow line in Fig. 2b) 
intensity distribution of the ball (red profile in Fig. 2b) suggests a hollow structure of 
the ball, which might be caused by the generation of gas inside the ball. As the bias 
was reversed to charge the battery, the ball shrank immediately (Fig. 2c), and the 
corresponding EDP shows the diffraction spots indexed as a body centered cubic (bcc) 
K metal (JCPDS card No. 65-4050) and the diffraction rings ascribed to the residual 
K2CO3, suggesting the electrochemical decomposition of K2CO3 to K metal (Fig. 2g). 
When the ball disappeared in the completely charged state (Fig. 2d), the EDP mainly 
shows diffraction spots indexed as K metal, implying the nearly complete 
decomposition of K2CO3 to K.  
Normally the solid electrolyte interface (SEI) layer should conduct ions but not 
electrons, therefore K+ should completely pass through the SEI film and return back 
to the metal K electrode after the charging process and no K metal should appear 
outside the SEI film. However, due to the electron tunneling effect or the generated 
SEI film may not be completely insulated, electrons may be able to tunnel through the 
SEI layer and combine with the K+
 before the latter crosses the SEI layer back to the 
K metal side, leading to the generation of a little metal K on the surface of CNT near 
the SEI film. Indeed, dark field image formed by choosing a diffraction spot from K 
(pink circle in the inset of Fig. S5a) shows unambiguously the presence of K on the 
surface of the CNT (Fig. S5b, bright areas). Similar result has also been reported by 
Lukas Medenbach et al. in sodium oxygen batteries [19].  
In potassium ion batteries with graphitic materials as electrode, K+ ions can 
electrochemically intercalate into the graphite [20]. In this study, the discharge was 
initiated from the intercalation of K+ ions into the CNT, and after discharging for a 
while, the intercalation was saturated, and then the K+ diffused along the surface of 
the CNT and reacted with CO2 in the TEM column, leading to the formation of 

=== PAGE 8 ===
7 
 
K2CO3 thus the expansion of the CNT (Fig. S6). After swelling of the CNT, the 
K2CO3 ball emerged from the CNT, K2O and CO2 triple point. The structure and 
composition of the discharge product on the surface of the CNT is essentially 
identical to that of the balls. 
For comparison, we also conducted experiments on K-CNT nanobattery under no 
gas condition, and we found that the CNT only expanded without generating any balls 
(Fig. S7, Movie S3). The above comparison experiments show clear evidence that the 
balls formed in the CNT cathode during discharge in the CO2 atmosphere was caused 
by the CO2-related electrochemical reaction. 
Composition analysis by electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) 
Low-loss EELS spectra from the discharged and partially charged balls show 
slightly different profiles (Fig. 2i, j). For the ball generated in discharge, it displays a 
major plasmon peak resided at 25.1 eV with a shoulder peak resided at 13.5 eV (Fig. 
2i, blue). Combining with the EDP result, we assign these two characteristic peaks to 
that of K2CO3. The low-loss spectrum from the partially charged ball is different from 
that of the discharged one, and the former shows multiple low-loss peaks resided at 
8.1, 13.5, 16.3, 25.1 and 31.2 eV (Fig. 2i, red). Beside the 13.5 eV and 25.1 eV peaks 
correspond to K2CO3, the other characteristic peaks resided at 8.1, 16.3 and 31.2 eV 
are originated from K metal. These peak positions and profiles showed excellent 
repeatability. Core-loss EELS spectra from both of the 1st discharged (Fig. 2j blue) 
and partially charged balls (Fig. 2j, red) show similar profiles, showing the presence 
of C, K and O. Combining with the EDP results, we assign the EELS characteristic 
peaks from the discharge product to that of K2CO3, while those peaks in charge state 
are from K2CO3 combining with K metal.        
Discussion 
When the CNT was negatively biased against the K counter electrode, the K+ could 
cross the solid electrolyte on the surface of metal K. Meanwhile the electrons were 
transported from the CNT to where it contacted the K2O solid electrolyte. When the 
K+ and the electrons met together (Fig. 3), the following two discharge reactions 
might take place:  
2K+ + 2e- + 2CO2 → K2CO3+ CO 
       (1) 
or 4K+ + 4e- + 2CO2 → K2CO3 + C         (2).  

=== PAGE 9 ===
8 
 
As such, there might be two discharge byproducts, i.e., CO from reaction (1), and C 
from reaction (2), respectively. Similar reaction pathways were proposed in the 
Li-CO2 and Na-CO2 batteries, but which reaction really took place is still under 
debate to date [21,22]. For the K-CO2 battery, the reaction mechanism is entirely 
unknown.  
To accurately identify the reaction product, we conducted an in-depth analysis of 
the discharge product. We found that plenty of nanobubbles (marked by yellow 
dashed lines in Fig. 4) were formed during the 1st discharge process (Fig. 4, Movie 
S4), suggesting the release of gas in the reaction, which is most likely CO (reaction 
1). 
The formation of “balloon” consisting of K2CO3 and its growth during discharge is 
interpreted the following way. When the CNT was negatively biased against the K 
counter electrode, the K+ crossed the solid electrolyte on the surface of metal K. 
Meanwhile the electrons were transported from the CNT to where it contacted the 
K2O solid electrolyte. And then the cathode reaction occurred as 2K+ + 2e- + 2CO2→ 
K2CO3 + CO at the three-phase junction (the contact point of the CNTs, K2O/K and 
CO2). The released CO gas inflates the as-formed K2CO3 sphere to form a hollow 
structure. Due to the continuous formation of K2CO3 and the releasing of CO, the 
sphere grows larger and larger. Similar result has been reported in Li-O2 battery by 
Wang et al. [3], in which the in-situ formed O2 gas also inflates the discharge products 
to form hollow structure (Fig. 3). 
In order to avoid the influence of the CNT, we used Ag nanowires as the air 
cathode for the discharge experiments. We found that there was no apparent carbon 
ball generated on the surface of the Ag NW (Fig. S8). Chen et al. reported that large 
carbon balls are generated on the surface of Ag NW [15]. However, we did not detect 
any carbon balls in our in-situ experiments, and the discrepancy between Chen’s 
results and ours may originate from the following facts: (1) Our experiment was 
carried out in an all solid environment, while their experiment was conducted in a 
liquid environment, in which the electrolyte may influence the discharge products. (2) 
In our experiments, the Ag electrode showed poor cyclability, which may be caused 
by the lack of carbon supply, as no carbon formation was observed during the 
discharge process. On the contrary, the carbon electrode showed much better 
cyclability than that of the Ag electrode, apparently due to the continuous carbon 
supply from the CNT during the charge process. Thus, our results suggest that the 

=== PAGE 10 ===
9 
 
discharge process should follow reaction (1).  
The charge reaction might occur via the following possible reaction routes: 
2K2CO3 + C → 4K + 3CO2 
 
     (3) 
or K2CO3→2K + CO2+ 1/2O2          (4). 
As such, understanding the decomposition of K2CO3 is important for exploring the 
charging mechanism of the K-CO2 battery. After charging of the K-CNT/CO2 
nanobattery, we found that the CNT was tapered, indicating carbon consumption 
during the charge process as shown reaction 3 (Fig. S9, Movie 5). The similar carbon 
consumption was also observed in Fig. 1, S2, Movies S1, S2.  
Based on the above discussion, the overall reaction of the K-CO2 battery is 
suggested to be [5,14,15,23,24]: 
Discharge:  2K + 2CO2 → K2CO3+CO 
       (5) 
Charge:    2K2CO3 + C → 4K + 3CO2 
       (6). 
During the discharge reaction, a hollow K2CO3 ball was formed. As the amount of 
potassium carbonate product increased and CO gas accumulated, causing the gradual 
inflating of the sphere. During charging, potassium carbonate was converted back to 
K, causing the shrinkage of the sphere. 
In our experiments, we consistently observed the inflation of the K2CO3 hollow 
balls during discharge and shrinkage of the balls during charge. Therefore in real 
K-CO2 battery design, how to accommodate the gas release during battery cycling 
must be considered. For example, appropriate ventilation system must be 
implemented to avoid gas release induced expansion of the battery.  
 
Conclusion 
In conclusion, we successfully conducted in-situ discharging/charging of a 
K-CNT/CO2 nanobattery inside an AC-ETEM. During discharge, K2CO3 hollow balls 
were formed via 2K+ + 2e- + 2CO2 → K2CO3+ CO, leading to the formation of plenty 
of nanobubbles in the CNT cathode. During charge, K2CO3 was decomposed back to 
K and CO2 via 2K2CO3 + C → 4K + 3CO2, leading to consumption of the CNT. Our 
results provide an in-depth understanding to the working mechanism of a K-CO2 
battery, which may lead to the design of K-CO2 batteries for energy storage 
applications.

an advanced aberration corrected environmental transmission electron 
microscope.  
 During discharge, K2CO3 was generated in the cathode, which converted back 
to metal K during charge, and the K-CO2 nanobattery demonstrates a good

 Moreover, it is evident that CO gas was released during discharge and the
