A B S T R A C T
In traditional aqueous electrolytes, Zinc ions intercalation induces a disordered phase transition in α-MnO2 
accompanied by severe manganese dissolution, leading to rapid collapse of the electrode structure. This study 
combines in-situ observation with multi-scale characterization through transmission electron microscopy tech­
niques, elucidating a design strategy for ionic liquid (x M Zn(OTF)2-EMIMOTF) electrolytes to modulate the 
structural evolution path of the α-MnO2 cathode in zinc-ion batteries, thereby effectively inhibiting its capacity 
decay caused by dissolution. Optimization of the ionic liquid concentration not only guides orderly zinc ions and 
protons intercalation reactions, promoting the conversion of α-MnO2 along a more stable path, but more 
importantly, it significantly inhibits manganese dissolution. Mechanistic studies show that the ionic liquid 
modulates local proton activity and solvation structure, changing the reaction kinetics and phase transition path 
of the cathode at the atomic scale, thereby achieving a significant improvement in battery cycle stability at the 
macroscopic level. This work not only reveals the critical role of ionic liquids in performance modulation but also 
provides a clear mechanistic basis and feasible material strategy for achieving controllable structural evolution of 
electrode materials through electrolyte design, which has important guiding significance for the development of 
high-performance, long-life multivalent ion batteries.
1. Introduction
Aqueous zinc-ion batteries [1] have attracted much attention for 
powering electronic devices and storage systems [2–4]. Manganese di­
oxide (MnO2), as a popular cathode material with various polymorphs 
(α-, β-, γ-, δ-MnO2) [5–8], is particularly attractive owing to its high 
capacity, natural abundance, and environmental advantages. Despite 
rapid progress in material design and performance optimization, the 
fundamental energy storage mechanism of α-MnO2||Zn batteries re­
mains highly debated. At the core of this debate lies the controversial 
role of charge carriers and reaction pathways at the MnO2 cathode [9]. 
Various reaction mechanisms have been proposed including (1) Zn2+
intercalation/deintercalation while maintaining the original tunnel 
configuration [10]; (2) Zn2+ insertion forming new phases such as spinel 
ZnMn2O4 and layered ZnxMnO2 at surface [11]; (3) conversion reaction 
between MnO2 and ZnMn2O4 without Zn2+ intercalation [12–14]; (4) 
H+ insertion forming new phases (MnOOH); (5) H+/Zn2+ co-insertion 
converting MnO2 to MnOOH and ZnMn2O4 [15].
To reconcile mixed experimental observations, a H+/Zn2+ co- 
insertion mechanism has been proposed, in which H+ governs rapid 
redox kinetics while Zn2+ participates in charge compensation, struc­
tural stabilization, or limited insertion at defects or near-surface regions, 
though whether Zn2+ truly enters the bulk lattice remains controversial 
[16]. Beyond insertion-type reactions and conversion mechanisms, 
increasing attention has been directed toward dissolution/deposition 
processes involving manganese species. During discharge, MnO2 can 
undergo partial conversion to MnOOH or dissolve into Mn2+ ions 
through proton-assisted reactions. These dissolved Mn2+ species may 
subsequently redeposit onto the cathode during charging, forming 
electrochemically active MnO2 
or related phases [17]. Such

We present a mixed salt-IL system [13,21,22] (Zn[OTF]2-EMIMOTF, 
1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium trifluoromethanesulfonate) which can 
provide Zn2+ and H+ ions. More details are shown in Fig. S1. Moreover, 
the in-situ experiments by ETEM conducted with this stable salt-RTIL 
system were performed to investigate the phase transformation of 
α-MnO2 at the microscopic scale. Due to the larger error associated with 
relatively similar diffraction patterns, the high-angle annular dark 
field-scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) tech­
nique was used for additional proof. By combining atomic-level electron 
microscopy, theoretical study, and macroscopic electrochemical tech­
niques, the study demonstrates the structural evolution of α-MnO2 
during Zn2+/H+ co-insertion. The ICP and Raman results reveal the 
MnO2/Mn2+ reaction storage mechanism of the zinc-ion battery. By 
correlating electrochemical behavior with structural, chemical, and 
interfacial evolution of the α-MnO2 cathode, this study aims to clarify 
the dominant reaction pathways and provide mechanistic insights for 
the development of stable and high-performance α-MnO2||Zn energy 
storage systems.
2. Results and discussion
2.1. The physicochemical properties of electrolytes
The interaction between cations and anions in ILs influences the 
solvation of zinc ions. Hydrogen bonds, Coulombic force, and van der 
Waals interactions have a considerable impact not only on the properties 
of the IL but also on the interactions between IL and salt Zn(OTF)2. A 
series of mixed salt-IL systems was presented (x M Zn[OTF]2 + EMI­
MOTF, x = 0.5, 0.8, 1, and 1.2) namely 0.5 M, 0.8 M, 1 M, and 1.2 M. The 
optical images of electrolytes are presented in Fig. S1a. Obviously, there 
is an upper limit of solubility of 1.2 M, so we focused our analysis on the 
remaining three electrolytes.
In the context of conventional chemical understanding, although the 
C(2)-H bond on the imidazole ring of EMIM+ possesses a certain degree 
of acidity (with a pKa of approximately 21-24), it is extremely difficult 
for it to undergo cleavage and dissociate into a free proton in the absence 
of strong bases or ultra-strong electric fields. For instance, one study 
[23] utilized CO2 as a trapping agent to instantly capture the highly 
unstable and transient N-heterocyclic carbene (NHC) intermediate 
generated upon the reductive dehydrogenation of EMIM+. Furthermore, 
by employing in situ surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) in 
conjunction with deuterium isotope tracing, researchers [24] were able 
to capture in real-time the conformational rotation of the imidazole 
cation driven by a strong interfacial electric field reaching magnitudes of 
up to 109 V m−1. They directly observed that, as the most labile C(2)-H 
bond approached the electrode surface, it exhibited a significant shift 
and formed strong hydrogen-bonding interactions, thereby capturing 
the complete microscopic process in which this chemical bond was 
weakened and ultimately activated toward cleavage under continuous 
polarization. Although it was not possible to directly prove the reduc­
ibility of the imidazole ring in isolation, we adopt a simple method [25,
26] to mimic the reaction mechanism occurring during the electro­
chemical process. 0.3 g of each IL (1 M-EOTF and pure-EOTF) was mixed 
with 0.15 M ratio of D2O and the mixtures were stirred with a magnetic 
stir bar for 30 min and immediately evacuated for approximately 24 h 
under vacuum at 60 ◦C to eliminate the impact of D2O. And the infrared 
spectrum of three sample named 1 M, 1 M-D, and pure-D is shown in 
Fig. S1b. According to the harmonic oscillator model, when hydrogen 
(1H) is replaced by deuterium (2D) with nearly twice the mass, the 
reduced mass increases, causing the infrared absorption peak to shift 
significantly toward lower wavenumbers. The pair of sharp double 
peaks at approximately 3116 cm−1 and 3160 cm−1 in the figure repre­
sent the C–H stretching vibrations that have not undergone deuteration, 
corresponding respectively to the C4,5-H on the ring and the C2–H. 
Comparison with 1 M and 1 M-D, a broad peak attributed to the C-D 
stretching vibration was observed, which proves that the hydrogen at 
the specific position of the imidazole ring (such as the C2 position) has 
been successfully deuterated to deuterium. Compared with 1 M-D and 
pure-D, it is obvious that the broad peak of C-D becomes very prominent 
and widened, and the absorption intensity significantly increases. This is 
because after introducing a 1 M concentration of the component, the 
C2–H much easier to occurred deuterium substitution which indirectly 
proves the activity of C2–H.

Phase transitions are classified into two categories based on changes in 
symmetry: reconstructive transition and displacive transition. The major 
difference between them lies in the presence of space group relationship 
[45–47] between the new phase and the original phase. As mentioned 
above, there is no subgroup-group relationship between I4/m and Pnma, 
indicating the phase form α-MnO2 to γ-MnO2 is belong to the recon­
structive transition with symmetry breaking and lattice distortion. The 
space group I4/m process a higher symmetry with 4 rotation axes (4), 
and mirror plane (m) along with c→direction, while the space group 
Pnma losses the 4 rotation axes (4) and increase the mirror plane along 
with three directions ( a→, b→, c→) [49–51]. Notably, the general contracted 
graph for I41/amd > Pnma (Fig. S21) provides possible paths between 
low symmetry (Pnma, ITA No 62) and a high-symmetry (I41/amd, ITA 
No 141) under an electrochemical environment. The index of Pnma in 
I41/amd equals the product of the indices for each one of the interme­
diate edges. As we know, the smaller the index, the lower the phase 
transition barrier. This is reason that we consider the subgroup-group 
relation between G = I41/amd and H= Pnma of the index [4] repre­
sented by green line, not only that there is an intermediate space group 
Z = Imma (ITA No. 74). This shows that the intermediate product 
γ-MnO2 is a subgroup of ZnMn2O4, which has higher symmetry, similar 
to the diffusional transformation type. Then the growth of the new 
ZnMn2O4 phase can describe that the atom arrangement of γ-MnO2 al­

the coexistence of polymorphic mixture (α-MnO2-γ-MnO2).

3. Conclusion
The study combines in-situ observation and multi-scale character­
ization using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to elucidate a 
design strategy for zinc-ion battery electrolytes based on ionic liquids, 
aiming to control the structural evolution pathway of the α-MnO2 
cathode and effectively slow down capacity degradation. The research 
reveals that the dominant storage mechanism is the conversion reaction 
caused by Zn2+/H+ co-insertion. And the ions intercalation will promote 
the α-MnO2/Mn2+ reaction, the combination of the ICP and spectros­
copy results explain that the amorphous state Mn2+(aq) is the final state 
for ions intercalation. Comparing the traditional aqueous electrolytes, 
ionic liquid-based electrolytes reduce the disordered phase transition in

MnO2, which is often accompanied by severe manganese dissolution and 
rapid electrode structure collapse. By optimizing the ionic liquid con­
centration, not only is the ordered intercalation of zinc ions promoted, 
guiding α-MnO2 along a more stable transformation pathway, but more 
importantly, it significantly inhibits manganese dissolution. Mechanistic 
studies show that the ionic liquid, by regulating local proton activity and 
solvation structure, alters the reaction kinetics and phase transition 
pathway of the cathode at the atomic scale, thereby achieving a signif­
icant improvement in battery cycling stability at the macroscopic level 
(at 60 mA g−1 the remain capacity at 177 mAh g−1 after 100 cycles). This 
study established a chain of "electrolyte composition, macroscopic per­
formance, micro-nano scale conversion pathway, and inhibition of 
dissolution", thereby clarifying the microscopic principle by which ionic 
liquids can enhance battery stability by regulating the kinetics of the 
positive electrode reaction. This provides a new design strategy for 
inhibiting α-MnO2 electrode dissolution.
