Metastable Solid Electrolytes: Synthesis, Stabilization Mechanisms and Ion Transport

Wang Pengzhan

Abstract: All-solid-state lithium batteries promise a step-change in safety and energy density, with the solid electrolyte as the enabling component. The field has long focused on thermodynamically stable phases, yet a distinct class—metastable solid electrolytes, crystalline phases that are non-ground-state at ambient pressure and room temperature but persist behind kinetic barriers—keeps drawing attention because metastability is frequently correlated with exceptionally high ionic conductivity. The attraction carries an intrinsic tension: once a metastable phase crosses its transformation barrier it collapses into the stable counterpart, and conductivity drops precipitously. This Mini-review examines that tension across sulfides (~60%), halides (~25%) and oxides (~15%), tracing four non-equilibrium routes to the metastable state—high-temperature quench, pressure release, mechanochemistry and interfacial confinement—and asking how the same open, disordered lattice that delivers high conductivity also courts instability.

Keywords: metastable solid electrolyte; ion transport; phase transition; kinetic stabilization; all-solid-state battery

1 Introduction

Replacing the flammable liquid electrolyte with a solid electrolyte is widely viewed as the definitive route to suppress lithium dendrites and thermal runaway[1]. Among the many SE chemistries, a recurring and striking observation is that several of the highest room-temperature ionic conductors owe their conducting phase not to the thermodynamic ground state but to a metastable crystal structure “frozen in” by kinetic barriers.

We take a metastable phase to mean a crystalline, ordered phase that is non-ground-state at ambient pressure and room temperature yet remains kinetically stabilized[2]. Two cases are explicitly excluded: nanocrystals whose structure is still the ground state merely shrunk in size, and defect-rich stable phases (point defects or disorder within a ground-state stoichiometry). The mnemonic is “ordered ≠ ground state”: only an ordered crystal that is not the ground state counts as metastable here.

Metastable phases arise through four canonical pathways. High-temperature quench freezes a high-temperature polymorph by rapid cooling, as in α-Li₃PS₄ retained by fast heating and quenching[2]. Pressure-release capture lets a high-pressure phase persist after decompression. Mechanochemical induction drives a non-equilibrium transformation to a metastable polymorph under ball-milling[3]. Epitaxial or interfacial confinement locks a metastable phase through strain at a film or heterointerface—the trace anti-perovskite Li₃OCl stabilized in a thin film is the clearest example[4].

Metastable phases must be kept distinct from amorphous or glassy states. Glasses such as Li₇P₃S₁₁ and amorphous LiPON are disordered networks that do not enter the competition among crystalline phases; they fail by crystallization, whereas a metastable crystal fails by phase transition to a stable polymorph[5,6]. The two are contrasted, never conflated.

One caveat on terminology: the “metastable” label for some ultra-high-conductivity sulfides is contested. For Li₁₀GeP₂S₁₂ (LGPS) and its analogues, some argue for a metastable, high-temperature-quenched product while others treat the phase as thermodynamically stabilizable. No absolute position is taken here; such cases are marked【controversial】 and the evidence is reported[7,8].

That opposition—high conductivity bought at the price of instability—runs through everything that follows.

2 Classification and Synthesis

2.1 Sulfides (~60%)

Sulfides dominate metastable SE research. Deiseroth et al.[9] introduced the Li₆PS₅X (X = Cl, Br, I) argyrodite family of crystalline Li superionic conductors, which grounds subsequent work on sulfide metastability and polymorphism.

The sharpest illustration of the conductivity–instability link is the Li₃PS₄ polymorphism. Miura et al.[2] combined in-situ synchrotron XRD resolved every 0.5 s with DFT to build temperature–size and time–temperature–transformation (TTT) diagrams. The ambient equilibrium phase is γ-Li₃PS₄; α is thermodynamically stable only above 480 °C. The conductivity gap is stark: σ_α exceeds 10⁻³ S cm⁻¹ at room temperature while σ_γ sits at 10⁻⁷ S cm⁻¹, four orders of magnitude lower. Its migration barrier, ≈0.18–0.22 eV from AIMD studies[31], reflects the open α framework. The metastable α phase is reached by heating an amorphous precursor at 400 °C min⁻¹ (243–374 °C, ~10 s) and quenching; slower heating gives β. Nanoconfinement explains the retention: α has the lowest surface energy (0.43 / 0.50 / 0.58 J m⁻² for α / β / γ), the lowest nucleation barrier at every temperature, and grain growth is too slow for the particle to escape the nanoscale. Bulk transition temperatures are γ→β ≈ 296 °C and β→α ≈ 385 °C, yet collapse to 127 °C and 180 °C at an 8.5 nm radius. The α phase is therefore a quantitative template for kinetic locking—and a warning that the same disorder enabling the conductivity is what makes the phase perishable.

Mechanochemistry offers a second non-equilibrium route; high-energy ball-milling directly induces the argyrodite metastable phase[3]. Low-temperature NMR places local Li⁺ mobility of argyrodites in the 10⁻²–10⁻³ S cm⁻¹ range[10,11] (precise values【evidence gap】). Ge substitution and halide exchange push conductivity toward ~10⁻² S cm⁻¹[12,13].

2.2 Halides (~25%)

Halide SEs, typified by Li₃MCl₆ (M = Y, In, Er, Sc…), have gained ground on high-voltage compatibility and good cathode matching. Asano et al.[14] reported cold-pressed Li₃YCl₆ and Li₃YBr₆ with room-temperature conductivity above 1 mS cm⁻¹ and 94% Coulombic efficiency in 4-V-class cells with LiCoO₂ and no coating; the per-composition split (≈0.5 mS cm⁻¹ for Li₃YCl₆, ≈1.7 mS cm⁻¹ for Li₃YBr₆, both ball-milled) is treated as community-level【evidence gap】 because the full text is not open-access.

The decisive factor for halides is polymorph-dependent transport. Li₃MX₆ adopts monoclinic C2/m, trigonal, orthorhombic Pnma and other polymorphs, each with distinct Li migration paths and barriers. The metastable or disordered polymorph accessed by high-temperature or fast-cooling routes tends to have the more open framework, the lower barrier and the higher conductivity—but also the greater susceptibility to transformation under heat or stress[15]. Li sublattice disorder (vacancy and occupancy) is the confirmed structural origin of high halide conductivity, the analogue of anion disorder in sulfides[16]. A deliberately “metastable chloride” electrolyte exploits this very property to improve formability for anode-free lithium-metal batteries[17].

Table 1 gathers the polymorph-dependent room-temperature conductivities reported in the primary literature. The spread is wide: ball-milled Li₃YCl₆ and Li₃YBr₆ reach ≈0.5 and ≈1.7 mS cm⁻¹[14]; water-mediated Li₃InCl₆ reaches ≈2.0 mS cm⁻¹ against ≈1.5 mS cm⁻¹ for the annealed form[30]; low-cost Li₂ZrCl₆ gives ≈0.4–0.8 mS cm⁻¹ ball-milled but collapses to ≈6×10⁻³ mS cm⁻¹ after annealing to the monoclinic form[25]. These are not synthesis artifacts—each route simply retains a different point on the metastable-stable spectrum, so for halides the polymorph retained matters as much as the composition. Activation energies and polymorph transition temperatures remain【evidence gap】 for most halides, since the primary reports are not open-access. And, as elsewhere, the disorder that lifts the conductivity is the disorder that makes the metastable polymorph the fragile one.

2.3 Oxides (~15%, trimmed)

Only anti-perovskites and metastable garnets are retained. Lithium-rich anti-perovskites such as Li₃OCl offer three-dimensional Li⁺ channels[18] but are thermodynamically unstable at room temperature and moisture-sensitive[19]. Turrell et al.[4] stabilized a trace metastable Li₃OCl phase in an RF-magnetron-sputtered film; FT-IR confirmed the absence of O–H, i.e. Li₃OCl rather than the stable hydroxy-halide Li₂OHCl. The phase decomposes into Li₂O + LiCl on moderate annealing, proving its metastability directly, though the film conductivity is only ~10⁻⁸ S cm⁻¹ (low phase purity). The result is double-edged: interfacial confinement can lock a metastable phase transiently, yet stabilization is hard and conductivity is poor. Strain and interfacial engineering can nonetheless raise Li diffusion in Li₃OCl[20].

Garnet-type Li₇La₃Zr₂O₁₂ (LLZO) has a metastable cubic high-conductivity phase; the tetragonal form is the low-temperature stable one and needs Al/Ta doping and fast cooling to persist[21,22]. High-entropy doping further improves stability and performance[23]. Sulfides, halides and oxides tell the same story: the metastable structure brings the conductivity and plants the instability.

3 Stabilization Mechanisms

3.1 Thermodynamic stabilization (altering the free-energy landscape)

Doping, substitution and high-entropy design reshape the phase diagram so the metastable phase gains relative stability or its competitors are suppressed. Ong et al.[8] quantified the free-energy relations of the LGPS family by first-principles, supplying the framework for any metastable-versus-ground-state assessment. In practice, oxygen doping raises the electrochemical stability of argyrodite[24]; aliovalent Al³⁺ substitution tunes both conductivity and stability of Li₂ZrCl₆[25]; and LLZO doping with high-entropy garnet design follows the same logic[22,23]. In every case the move that stabilizes the phase also tends to blunt the very disorder responsible for fast conduction.

3.2 Kinetic locking (raising the transition barrier)

Where thermodynamics cannot yet be bent, kinetic locking raises the barrier standing between the metastable and stable states. Quenching and ball-milling act directly: fast heating–quenching creates α-Li₃PS₄, fast cooling traps cubic LLZO, and mechanochemistry generates argyrodite[2,3,22]. Nanoconfinement is a second lever—α-Li₃PS₄ is thermodynamically stable only at the nanoscale because of its low surface energy, and its transition temperatures fall to 127 and 180 °C at an 8.5 nm radius[2]. Interfacial strain tunes the same competition: Lin et al. showed strain modulating Li diffusion and conductivity in Li₃OCl[20]. A fourth route, epitaxial or thin-film confinement, is what allows RF sputtering to freeze a trace of metastable Li₃OCl into a film[4]. Coatings complete the set by suppressing the cathode/electrolyte side reactions that would otherwise open a chemical path back to the stable phase. Kinetic locking is the workhorse of metastable-phase retention, but it lasts only as long as the thermal and mechanical history it endures—cyclability over thousands of hours remains the open question.

4 Ion Transport and Interfacial Challenges

4.1 Structural origin of high conductivity

The causal chain for metastable high-conductivity phases is consistent: metastable structure → anion/cation sublattice disorder plus open framework → low Li⁺ migration barrier → ultrahigh σ. In argyrodites, anion (S/Cl) disorder and rotational rearrangement are tied by ³¹P NMR to superionic conductivity[26], and anion disorder lowering the Li⁺ barrier is supported by AIMD and machine-learning potentials[27]; in halides, Li sublattice disorder plays the same role[16]. The principle is made visible by in-situ high-resolution TEM: Cu₂₋ₓSe undergoes a vacancy-ordered to superionic transition at ~140 °C, and cation-sublattice disordering opens multiple interstitial sites for superionic conduction[28]. Cu₂₋ₓSe is non-lithium, yet its “disorder → superionic” pathway is a general mechanism and a direct corroboration of metastable high conductivity. The openness that makes the conductor fast is, again, the openness that lets it fall apart.

4.2 Failure by phase transition

The fatal weakness of a metastable phase is transition failure. α-Li₃PS₄, once heated across the barrier back to γ, falls from >10⁻³ to 10⁻⁷ S cm⁻¹—four orders[2]. The Li₃OCl film decomposes straight into Li₂O + LiCl on annealing, losing the conducting phase entirely[4]. Halide polymorphs are expected to undergo the same high-conductivity-to-low-conductivity conversion under thermal or stress history【evidence gap】. The boundaries of failure—temperature, grain size, stress—are precisely what kinetic locking must define.

4.3 Interfacial challenges: stress- and side-reaction-induced premature transition

Even with the bulk phase locked, interfaces remain the bottleneck: sulfides suffer side reactions at cathode and Li-metal interfaces, halides must be made compatible with high-voltage cathodes, and oxide films show low phase purity and limited conductivity[4,29]. Beyond the usual impedance growth, interfaces host two dedicated pathways by which a metastable phase is driven to transform prematurely—at conditions far milder than its bulk transition temperature.

Interfacial stress as a mechanical trigger. Metastable retention is itself an act of kinetic locking: nanoconfinement, epitaxial strain and grain-size control keep the phase trapped behind a barrier[2,4]. Cycling-induced volume change, Li-dendrite impingement and constrained thin-film geometry generate localized mechanical stress that lowers the effective transformation barrier by biasing the free-energy landscape and, at the same time, seeds heterogeneous nucleation of the stable polymorph at the interface. A phase stable in the bulk can therefore nucleate its stable counterpart locally at the SE|cathode or SE|Li interface well below the bulk transition temperature—the 296–385 °C values for Li₃PS₄ become irrelevant once stress supplies the extra driving force. Lin et al.[20] showed strain itself modulating Li diffusivity and conductivity in Li₃OCl, confirming that mechanical state and phase stability are coupled.

Side reactions as a chemical trigger. Chemical or electrochemical side reactions rewrite the local chemistry—composition, oxygen and water activity, interfacial potential—so that the metastable phase is no longer even kinetically favored, or so that a new phase templates the stable polymorph. The anti-perovskite Li₃OCl is the clearest exemplar: surface hydrolysis (O–H incorporation) converts it into the thermodynamically stable hydroxy-halide Li₂OHCl[4]—the stable counterpart is reached not by heating but by a side reaction. The same corrosion is expected for halides at the Li-metal interface (reductive decomposition) and for sulfides under high-voltage oxidation, where the product can nucleate the stable sulfide polymorph. Stress and side reaction act in concert: stress opens the pathway, chemistry supplies the pull, and the metastable phase yields at the interface long before the bulk would.

Mitigating premature transition thus demands mechanical and chemical stabilization together—coatings that suppress side reactions[4,29], strain-engineered buffer layers, and kinetic-locking routes robust to mechanical load.

5 Current Limitations and Outlook

Three gaps stand between the current picture and a usable metastable electrolyte.

5.1 In-situ characterization tracking phase transitions. Studies still lean on post-hoc structural assignment and lack real-time tracking of the metastable phase from synthesis to service. Coupling TTT diagrams[2] with in-situ TEM of order–disorder transitions[28], and pushing time-resolved synchrotron XRD and spectroscopy to higher resolution, is the way to map the lock–unlock critical conditions.

5.2 High-throughput prediction of metastable phases. The metastable search space is vast and trial-and-error is expensive. Building on phase-diagram and free-energy-surface calculations[8] together with machine-learning potentials and high-throughput screening, one can predict at the design stage which compositions and polymorphs offer a “low-barrier, high-conductivity, lockable” window to steer experiments.

5.3 Scalable, low-energy synthesis. Quenching and ball-milling face energy and consistency barriers at scale. Water- and acid-mediated synthesis of phase-pure Li₃InCl₆ and related halides points to a scalable, low-cost, air- and water-tolerant route[30]; mechanochemistry retains low-energy promise[3]. Keeping the metastable window open under low energy input is the practical test of every strategy above.

Conclusion

Metastable solid electrolytes open a distinct route to a higher conductivity ceiling, held together by a single insight: the open, disordered framework that delivers high conductivity is the same framework that courts instability. The conceptual boundaries set out here—four origins, two stabilization families, one persistent dichotomy—form a scaffold for reading these materials. Only by decoupling yet combining thermodynamic stabilization and kinetic locking, and by using in-situ characterization and computation to hold a scalably maintainable metastable window, can these “frozen high-conductivity phases” reach all-solid-state batteries.
